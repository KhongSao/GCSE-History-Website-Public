Settlement & migration

Overview

Key dates

  • 1830 — Indian Removal Act — US government begins forcible displacement of Native Americans
  • 1840s — Oregon Trail migration — mass westward movement begins
  • 1862 — Homestead Act — free land offered to settlers willing to farm the Plains
  • 1869 — Transcontinental Railroad completed — transformed the Plains forever
  • 1890 — Wounded Knee Massacre — symbolic end of the Indian Wars

Key people

  • President Andrew Jackson — Signed Indian Removal Act 1830 — set the pattern of forced displacement
  • Sitting Bull — Hunkpapa Lakota leader — symbol of Native resistance
  • General Custer — Led 7th Cavalry — defeated at Little Bighorn 1876; became a symbol of the Indian Wars

Key terms

  • Manifest Destiny — Belief that American expansion westward was God-given and inevitable
  • Homesteader — Settler farming the Great Plains under the Homestead Act
  • Plains Indians — Native peoples of the Great Plains — nomadic, buffalo-dependent
  • Reservation — Defined land parcels where Native Americans were confined

Key events

  • Homestead Act (1862) — At the time: opened the Plains to mass settlement — Long term: drove conflict with Native Americans
  • Transcontinental Railroad (1869) — At the time: connected East to West — Long term: accelerated buffalo slaughter and Native displacement
  • Indian Wars (1860s–90s) — At the time: series of military conflicts — Long term: ended Native American independence on the Plains

Key figures

  • 2,000 miles — Length of the Oregon Trail — the primary route west
  • ~600,000 — Native American population around 1800 — halved by 1900
  • 160 acres — Land granted to homesteaders under the Homestead Act 1862

Geography of the Plains

Key dates

  • 1840s — First wagon trains cross the Plains — seen as a wasteland to pass through, not settle
  • 1869 — Transcontinental Railroad — crosses the Plains and changes perception entirely

Key people

  • Plains Indians — Adapted perfectly to the Plains — nomadic life followed the buffalo
  • Early migrants — Treated the Plains as a barrier — the change to viewing it as desirable land is key

Key terms

  • Great Plains — Vast grassland stretching from Mississippi to the Rockies — the central geography of this unit
  • Treeless Prairie — No timber for building or fuel — a major problem for early settlers
  • Grassland — Supported vast buffalo herds and Plains Indian nomadic life
  • Sod — Compressed earth used for building "sodbusters'" homes — adaptation to lack of timber

Key events

  • First crossings (1840s) — Oregon Trail migrants cross the Plains — at the time: obstacle; later: territory to settle
  • Transcontinental Railroad (1869) — At the time: crossed the Plains — Long term: made the Plains accessible and attractive to settlers

Key figures

  • 500,000 sq miles — Size of the Great Plains — vast, treeless, seemingly inhospitable
  • Under 20 inches — Annual rainfall — why early settlers called it the "Great American Desert"
  • -40°C to 43°C — Extreme temperature range — one of the core challenges for homesteaders

Push or Pull Me West

Key dates

  • 1840s — Oregon Trail at peak use — religious and economic push/pull factors driving migration
  • 1848 — Gold Rush — pull factor of enormous power draws 80,000 in one year
  • 1862 — Homestead Act — government-created pull factor; free land

Key people

  • Pioneer families — Driven by combination of push and pull — students must be able to distinguish both
  • President Lincoln — Signed Homestead Act — turned land into the ultimate pull factor

Key terms

  • Push factors — Reasons people left: poverty, unemployment, religious persecution, land exhaustion
  • Pull factors — Reasons people moved west: free land, gold, religious freedom, adventure
  • Manifest Destiny — Ideological pull — religious and national duty to settle the continent
  • Oregon Trail — 2,000-mile route west — the physical embodiment of push/pull migration

Key events

  • Oregon Trail migration (1840s) — At the time: religious/economic migration — Long term: opened the West to permanent settlement
  • Gold Rush (1848) — At the time: massive pull factor — Long term: California's population explosion; westward momentum
  • Homestead Act (1862) — At the time: free land — Long term: accelerated displacement of Plains Indians

Key figures

  • 300,000 — Used Oregon Trail by 1870 — scale of voluntary migration west
  • 2,000 miles — Length of Oregon Trail — the journey itself was a push/pull test
  • $10 — Filing fee under Homestead Act — low barrier to land ownership

Donners, Sagers and Goulds

Key dates

  • 1844 — Sager family cross the Plains — parents die; children orphaned on the trail
  • 1846–47 — Donner Party disaster — snowed in Sierra Nevada; survival through cannibalism

Key people

  • George Donner — Leader of Donner Party — poor decision-making led to disaster
  • Sager children — Seven orphaned children — used as a case study for hardships of migration

Key terms

  • Oregon Trail — The route these families took — context for the hardships
  • Overlanders — Emigrants crossing the continent by wagon — the broader group these families represent
  • Wagon train — The method of travel — strength in numbers, but still enormously dangerous

Key events

  • Donner Party (1846–47) — At the time: catastrophic failure — Long term: cautionary tale; shaped perception of westward migration
  • Sager orphaning (1844) — At the time: individual tragedy — Long term: illustrates the human cost of migration

Key figures

  • 87 — People in Donner Party — 48 survived
  • 7 — Sager children orphaned on the trail — ages 5 months to 14 years
  • ~2,000 miles — Oregon Trail length — the danger was ever-present across the whole journey

Manifest Destiny

Key dates

  • 1845 — Term "Manifest Destiny" coined by John O'Sullivan
  • 1846 — Oregon Treaty — USA gains Pacific Northwest from Britain
  • 1846–48 — Mexican-American War — USA gains California, Texas, New Mexico
  • 1853 — Gadsden Purchase — final piece of continental expansion

Key people

  • John O'Sullivan — Coined "Manifest Destiny" — gave ideology its name and legitimacy
  • President Polk — Most expansionist president — oversaw the war with Mexico and Oregon Treaty

Key terms

  • Manifest Destiny — Belief that American expansion west was God-given and inevitable — justification for displacement of Native Americans
  • Expansionism — USA's policy of territorial growth — underpinned by Manifest Destiny
  • Providence — Religious dimension — God willed American expansion across the continent
  • Sea to shining sea — Popular expression of the goal — coast-to-coast USA

Key events

  • Oregon Treaty (1846) — At the time: peaceful resolution with Britain — Long term: secured Pacific coast for USA
  • Mexican-American War (1846–48) — At the time: military expansion — Long term: gave USA California (Gold Rush territory)
  • Gadsden Purchase (1853) — At the time: completed continental USA — Long term: confirmed Manifest Destiny as achieved

Key figures

  • 3 million sq miles — Added to USA between 1800–1853 — territory doubled
  • 1845 — Year the ideology was formally named — but the idea predates it

The Miners

Key dates

  • January 1848 — Gold discovered at Sutter's Mill, California
  • 1849 — 49ers arrive in mass numbers — California Gold Rush at its peak
  • 1859 — Comstock Lode silver discovered in Nevada — mining spreads east onto Plains

Key people

  • John Sutter — Owner of the mill where gold was found — ironically ruined by the rush
  • President Polk — Officially announced the gold discovery — triggered the rush

Key terms

  • Gold Rush — Mass migration triggered by gold discovery — the defining pull factor of the late 1840s
  • 49ers — Miners who arrived in 1849 — the bulk of the first rush
  • Placer mining — Panning for surface gold in rivers — accessible to individuals, quickly exhausted
  • Vigilantes — Self-appointed law enforcers in mining camps — fill the gap left by absent government
  • Comstock Lode — Major silver discovery (1859) — brought mining deeper into the West

Key events

  • Gold discovered (Jan 1848) — At the time: triggered mass migration — Long term: California's explosive growth; gateway to further westward expansion
  • 49ers arrive (1849) — At the time: lawless boom towns — Long term: permanence of settlement in California
  • Comstock Lode (1859) — At the time: new rush to Nevada — Long term: mining as permanent feature of western economy

Key figures

  • 80,000 — 49ers arrived in California in 1849 alone
  • $800 million — Gold extracted from California in total
  • 14,000 → 100,000 — California population in just two years — the fastest population growth in US history

The Mormons and Joseph Smith

Key dates

  • 1830 — Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints founded by Joseph Smith
  • 1844 — Joseph Smith killed by a mob in Carthage, Illinois
  • 1847 — Brigham Young leads Mormons to Great Salt Lake, Utah

Key people

  • Joseph Smith — Founded the Mormon Church — his murder triggered the westward migration
  • Brigham Young — Led the migration to Utah — built a thriving community from almost nothing

Key terms

  • Mormon — Member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints — key group for this unit
  • Polygamy — Practice of multiple wives — the main source of hostility from non-Mormons
  • Nauvoo — Mormon city in Illinois — where persecution intensified, leading to the migration

Key events

  • Church founded (1830) — At the time: small new religious movement — Long term: became one of America's most significant religious communities
  • Smith killed (1844) — At the time: sparked migration crisis — Long term: Brigham Young leads Mormons to Utah, creating a permanent community
  • Migration to Utah (1847) — At the time: religious exodus — Long term: established a thriving community in an "empty" territory

Key figures

  • 26,000 — Mormon Church members by 1844 — significant community size
  • 148 — Mormons in first pioneer group to Utah — small but determined vanguard

Brigham Young Thriving in the West

Key dates

  • 1847 — Mormons arrive at Great Salt Lake — begin building their community
  • 1850s — Salt Lake City established and grows rapidly
  • 1857 — Mountain Meadows Massacre — darkest moment of Mormon history
  • 1869 — Transcontinental Railroad reaches Utah — breaks Mormon isolation

Key people

  • Brigham Young — Organised and led the community — practical genius as well as religious leader

Key terms

  • Irrigation — Mormon innovation — turned desert into farmland; key reason for their success
  • Theocracy — Government by religious leaders — Brigham Young ran Utah as a religious state
  • Salt Lake City — The Mormon capital — built from scratch in the desert
  • Deseret — Brigham Young's name for the Mormon territory — meaning "honeybee" (industry)
  • Polygamy — Continued practice under Young — caused friction with US government

Key events

  • Arrival in Utah (1847) — At the time: desperate refuge — Long term: foundation of a thriving, self-sufficient community
  • Mountain Meadows Massacre (1857) — At the time: 120 emigrants killed — Long term: strained Mormon relationship with US government
  • Railroad reaches Utah (1869) — At the time: ended Mormon isolation — Long term: integrated Mormons into wider US society

Key figures

  • 70,000 — Mormons had migrated to Utah by 1869 — from 148 in 1847

Mountain Meadows Massacre

Key dates

  • September 1857 — Mountain Meadows Massacre — Mormon militia kill emigrant wagon train
  • 1877 — John D. Lee executed — only person convicted after 20 years

Key people

  • Brigham Young — Alleged involvement never proven — ordered "let them pass" but too late
  • John D. Lee — Mormon militia leader — executed 1877; made scapegoat for the massacre

Key terms

  • Mountain Meadows Massacre — Attack on civilian wagon train by Mormon militia — most controversial event in Mormon history
  • Fancher Party — The emigrant wagon train that was attacked — travelling to California
  • Utah War — Tension between US government and Mormons in 1857 — context for the massacre

Key events

  • Mountain Meadows Massacre (Sept 1857) — At the time: covered up by Mormon community — Long term: damaged Mormon reputation; complicated Utah statehood
  • John D. Lee executed (1877) — At the time: one conviction after 20 years — Long term: scapegoated; Brigham Young's role never fully investigated

Key figures

  • 120 — Members of Fancher Party killed — men, women and most children
  • 17 — Children under 8 who survived — spared as too young to remember
  • 20 years — Gap before any conviction — shows how covered up the massacre was

Homesteaders - Push-Pull

Key dates

  • 1862 — Homestead Act signed by Lincoln — free land to settlers
  • 1865 — End of Civil War — veterans head west; Exodusters begin moving
  • 1869 — Transcontinental Railroad — made the Plains accessible

Key people

  • President Lincoln — Signed the Homestead Act — a political and economic act with enormous consequences
  • Exodusters — Black homesteaders — pushed by Southern racism; pulled by free land

Key terms

  • Homestead Act — 1862 law giving 160 acres free to settlers — the central pull factor for homesteaders
  • Push factors — Poverty, Civil War devastation, persecution — drove people westward
  • Pull factors — Free land, fresh start, religious freedom, railroad connections
  • Exodusters — Black Americans who moved west after Civil War — seeking escape from racism and sharecropping
  • Sodbuster — Nickname for Plains homesteaders — had to break the tough sod to farm

Key events

  • Homestead Act (1862) — At the time: drew thousands of settlers — Long term: accelerated conflict with Plains Indians over land
  • End of Civil War (1865) — At the time: freed veterans and enslaved people — Long term: Exodusters move west; homesteading booms
  • Transcontinental Railroad (1869) — At the time: made the journey possible — Long term: transformed the Plains economy

Key figures

  • 160 acres — Offered free under Homestead Act — powerful pull factor
  • 5 years — Residency required to claim land — the commitment expected
  • 400,000 families — Claimed land under the Homestead Act — scale of the response

Homesteaders - Problems and Solutions

Key dates

  • 1874 — Barbed wire patented by Joseph Glidden — solved the fencing problem
  • 1870s–80s — Peak homesteading era — problems and solutions most visible here

Key people

  • Joseph Glidden — Invented/patented barbed wire (1874) — transformed the Plains landscape
  • John Deere — Developed the steel plough — enabled homesteaders to break the tough sod

Key terms

  • Dugout — Home built into a hillside — adaptation to lack of timber and need for insulation
  • Sodbuster — Homesteader who broke the tough Plains sod — named after their primary challenge
  • Dry farming — Technique for farming with minimal water — long-term solution to drought
  • Barbed wire — Solved the fencing problem without timber — At the time: practical; Long term: ended open range
  • Wind pump — Used to access underground water — technological solution to drought

Key events

  • Invention of barbed wire (1874) — At the time: solved fencing and land disputes — Long term: ended the open range era; Cowboys vs Homesteaders conflict
  • Introduction of drought-resistant crops — At the time: practical survival — Long term: made Plains farming viable

Key figures

  • 40% — Proportion of homestead claims successfully completed — shows how hard it was
  • Under 20 inches — Annual rainfall — explains the constant drought problem
  • -40°C to 43°C — Temperature extremes — explains the need for dugout homes and adaptations

The Plains Indians & the Indian Wars

The Plains Indians

Key dates

  • Pre-1840 — Plains Indian lifestyle fully established — nomadic, buffalo-dependent, spiritual
  • 1830 — Indian Removal Act — first major US government threat to Native land
  • 1851 — Fort Laramie Treaty — government attempts to contain Plains Indians

Key people

  • Sitting Bull — Hunkpapa Lakota Sioux chief — spiritual and political leader of resistance
  • Crazy Horse — Oglala Lakota war leader — key figure at Little Bighorn

Key terms

  • Tipi — Portable home — perfectly adapted to nomadic life following buffalo
  • Nomadic — Way of life — following buffalo herds across the Plains
  • Band — Small family group within a tribe — basic social unit
  • Great Spirit — Spiritual belief — everything in nature has a spirit; land cannot be owned
  • Counting Coup — Touching an enemy in battle — bravery more valued than killing

Key events

  • Indian Removal Act (1830) — At the time: removed Eastern tribes west — Long term: set precedent for later dispossession
  • Fort Laramie Treaty (1851) — At the time: defined tribal territories — Long term: increasingly violated by US government

Key figures

  • ~250,000 — Plains Indians in the 1850s — across multiple nations
  • 7 — Main tribal groups on the Great Plains — Sioux, Cheyenne, Arapaho etc.

Native Life - The Buffalo

Key dates

  • Pre-1840 — Buffalo central to all aspects of Plains Indian life
  • 1870s — Mass slaughter of buffalo begins — hide hunters and railroad construction
  • ~1889 — Buffalo nearly extinct — fewer than 1,000 remain

Key people

  • Buffalo Bill (Cody) — Symbol of the buffalo hunters — killed ~4,000 buffalo; celebrated, not condemned, at the time
  • General Sherman — Encouraged buffalo slaughter as policy — "kill the buffalo, kill the Indians"

Key terms

  • Buffalo — The foundation of Plains Indian life — food, shelter, clothing, tools, spirituality
  • Nomadic — Life organised around following buffalo herds — understanding this explains everything else
  • Sacred — Buffalo was a spiritual gift, not just a resource — explains horror at white slaughter

Key events

  • Mass buffalo slaughter (1870s–80s) — At the time: destroyed the economic base of Plains Indian life — Long term: forced Plains Indians onto reservations; deliberately used as a weapon of policy
  • Railroad construction (1860s) — At the time: workers hunted buffalo for food — Long term: accelerated the collapse

Key figures

  • ~30 million — Buffalo on the Plains in 1800 — healthy, sustainable population
  • ~1,000 — Buffalo remaining by 1889 — near extinction in under 20 years
  • 100+ — Different uses Plains Indians had for the buffalo — food, shelter, tools, clothing, spiritual

Native Life - Visions and Land

Key dates

  • Pre-contact — Spiritual beliefs established — vision quests, land ownership concepts, Great Spirit
  • 1851 — Fort Laramie Treaty — US government imposes land boundaries that clash with Native beliefs

Key people

  • Sitting Bull — Famous for his visions — spiritual authority as important as political leadership
  • Medicine men — Spiritual leaders — their suppression was part of the cultural destruction of Plains Indians

Key terms

  • Vision quest — Spiritual ritual — young men seek guidance through isolation, fasting, prayer
  • Great Spirit (Wakan Tanka) — Supreme spiritual force — everything in nature is sacred and connected
  • Sacred land — Land cannot be owned — fundamental clash with white settler concepts of property
  • Medicine man — Spiritual leader of a band — healer, advisor, interpreter of visions
  • Sun Dance — Major communal spiritual ceremony — reinforced tribal unity

Key events

  • Fort Laramie Treaty (1851) — At the time: imposed land boundaries — Long term: fundamentally incompatible with Native belief that land cannot be owned
  • Vision quests and ceremonies — At the time: central to Plains Indian identity — Long term: suppressed under reservation system; destruction of culture

Key figures

  • Up to 4 days — Duration of a vision quest — shows the depth of spiritual commitment

The Permanent Indian Frontier

Key dates

  • 1830 — Indian Removal Act — Eastern tribes forcibly moved west of Mississippi
  • 1834 — Permanent Indian Frontier established — all land west of Mississippi declared Indian Territory
  • 1840s — Oregon Trail migration begins — immediately undermines the "permanent" frontier

Key people

  • President Andrew Jackson — Signed the Indian Removal Act — personally hostile to Native Americans

Key terms

  • Permanent Indian Frontier — The line drawn in 1834 — supposedly permanent; ignored within a decade
  • Indian Territory — Land set aside for Native Americans — concept destroyed by westward expansion
  • Indian Removal Act — 1830 law forcing Eastern tribes west — established the pattern of forced displacement
  • Reservation — The replacement for Indian Territory — smaller, defined parcels of land

Key events

  • Indian Removal Act (1830) — At the time: cleared Eastern tribes west — Long term: set the precedent for all future displacement
  • Permanent Indian Frontier (1834) — At the time: a supposed guarantee — Long term: broken almost immediately; shows US policy was never truly "permanent"
  • Oregon Trail migration (1840s) — At the time: crossed "Indian Territory" — Long term: made the Permanent Frontier meaningless

Key figures

  • All land — West of the Mississippi declared "Indian Territory" in 1834 — a promise quickly broken

The Fort Laramie Treaty (1851)

Key dates

  • 1851 — Fort Laramie Treaty signed — US government attempts to contain Plains Indians by defining tribal territories
  • 1868 — Second Fort Laramie Treaty — US forced to negotiate after Red Cloud's War; promised Native lands
  • 1874 — Gold discovered in Black Hills — Fort Laramie 1868 broken almost immediately

Key people

  • Red Cloud — Oglala Sioux chief — won Red Cloud's War; forced US to sign 1868 treaty; rare Native military victory
  • Senator Henry Dawes — Later architect of the Dawes Act — continued the pattern of breaking Native land agreements

Key terms

  • Fort Laramie Treaty — Agreement defining tribal territories and promising annuities — broken repeatedly by US government
  • Annuities — Annual payments of goods promised to tribes — stopped being paid when convenient
  • Great Sioux Reservation — Land guaranteed to Lakota Sioux under 1868 treaty — violated by gold rush
  • Bozeman Trail — Route through Sioux territory — US army tried to protect it; flashpoint for conflict

Key events

  • Fort Laramie Treaty 1851 — At the time: defined tribal territories across the Plains — Long term: violated repeatedly as settlers moved west
  • Fort Laramie Treaty 1868 — At the time: US concession to Red Cloud — Long term: broken within years when gold found in Black Hills

Key figures

  • 10,000+ — Native Americans attended the 1851 treaty negotiations — shows the scale of the agreement
  • $50,000 — Annual annuities promised to tribes under the 1851 treaty — financial bribe for compliance

Indian Wars - Little Crow 1862, Cheyenne Uprising 1863, Red Cloud 1868

Key dates

  • 1862 — Little Crow's War (Dakota War) — Dakota Sioux rose against broken treaty promises
  • 1863 — Cheyenne Uprising — conflict following encroachment on Cheyenne hunting grounds
  • 1866–68 — Red Cloud's War — Sioux resistance to Bozeman Trail; ended in Native victory
  • 1868 — Second Fort Laramie Treaty — US conceded to Red Cloud; abandoned forts

Key people

  • Little Crow — Dakota Sioux chief — led 1862 uprising after government broke annuity promises; killed 1863
  • Red Cloud — Oglala Sioux — won Red Cloud's War; forced US abandonment of Bozeman Trail forts
  • President Lincoln — Authorised mass execution of 38 Dakota after Little Crow's War

Key terms

  • Little Crow's War — Dakota Sioux uprising 1862 — caused by broken annuity payments and starvation
  • Red Cloud's War — Sioux resistance to Bozeman Trail 1866–68 — one of the few Native American military successes
  • Bozeman Trail — Route through Sioux territory to Montana goldfields — flashpoint of Red Cloud's War

Key events

  • Little Crow's War (1862) — At the time: violent uprising in Minnesota — Long term: led to mass executions and removal of Dakota Sioux
  • Red Cloud's War (1866–68) — At the time: closed the Bozeman Trail — Long term: led to Fort Laramie Treaty 1868; showed US could be defeated

Key figures

  • ~500 — Settlers killed in Little Crow's War — largest loss of civilian life in 19th-century Indian Wars
  • 38 — Dakota warriors hanged after Little Crow's War — largest mass execution in US history
  • 81 — US soldiers killed at Fetterman Trap 1866 — part of Red Cloud's War

Indian Wars - Sand Creek 1864 and Fetterman Trap 1866

Key dates

  • 1864 — Sand Creek Massacre — Colorado militia attacked peaceful Cheyenne village
  • 1866 — Fetterman Trap — all 81 soldiers in Fetterman's command ambushed and killed
  • 1868 — Fort Laramie Treaty — US forced to negotiate after Red Cloud's War successes

Key people

  • Colonel John Chivington — Led Sand Creek Massacre — acted against orders; later investigated by Congress
  • Captain William Fetterman — Boasted he could defeat all Sioux with 80 men — killed along with exactly 80 soldiers
  • Red Cloud — Oglala Sioux war leader — masterminded resistance including Fetterman Trap

Key terms

  • Sand Creek Massacre — November 1864 — Colorado militia attacked Cheyenne flying a US flag and white flag of peace
  • Fetterman Trap — December 1866 — Sioux lured Fetterman's force into an ambush; all 81 killed
  • Red Cloud's War — 1866–68 — Sioux resistance campaign that included the Fetterman Trap
  • Bozeman Trail — Route to Montana gold fields through Sioux territory — cause of the conflict

Key events

  • Sand Creek Massacre (1864) — At the time: celebrated by some white Americans — Long term: caused outrage in Congress; fuelled further Native resistance
  • Fetterman Trap (1866) — At the time: shock military defeat — Long term: forced US to negotiate Fort Laramie Treaty 1868

Key figures

  • ~150–200 — Cheyenne and Arapaho killed at Sand Creek — mostly women, children and elderly
  • 81 — US soldiers killed at Fetterman Trap — a complete military disaster for the US Army

Battle of the Little Bighorn (1876)

Key dates

  • 1874 — Gold discovered in Black Hills — violated Fort Laramie Treaty 1868; triggered the Great Sioux War
  • 1876 — Great Sioux War — US Army campaign to force Sioux onto reservations
  • 25–26 June 1876 — Battle of the Little Bighorn — Custer's Last Stand
  • 1877 — Crazy Horse surrenders; Sitting Bull flees to Canada — end of major resistance

Key people

  • General George Custer — Led 7th Cavalry — recklessly attacked; killed at Little Bighorn; became a symbol used to justify vengeance
  • Sitting Bull — Spiritual and military leader — organised the defence; fled to Canada after the battle
  • Crazy Horse — War leader — directed the fighting at Little Bighorn; surrendered 1877

Key terms

  • Little Bighorn — River in Montana — site of Custer's defeat; the high point of Native resistance
  • 7th Cavalry — Custer's regiment — destroyed at Little Bighorn
  • Black Hills — Sacred Sioux land — violation of Fort Laramie Treaty 1868 by US gold miners
  • Great Sioux War — 1876–77 US military campaign — triggered by gold rush into Black Hills

Key events

  • Gold rush in Black Hills (1874) — At the time: violated Fort Laramie Treaty — Long term: triggered Great Sioux War and Little Bighorn
  • Battle of Little Bighorn (1876) — At the time: greatest Native American military victory — Long term: US responded with overwhelming force; led to final defeat of the Sioux

Key figures

  • 268 — US soldiers killed at Little Bighorn — including Custer and his entire command
  • ~1,800–2,000 — Estimated Sioux and Cheyenne warriors at Little Bighorn — vastly outnumbered Custer
  • ~60–100 — Estimated Native American casualties — low compared to US losses

The Dawes Act (1887)

Key dates

  • 1887 — Dawes Act passed — broke up tribal reservation land into individual allotments
  • 1934 — Indian Reorganization Act — partially reversed Dawes Act; showed the destruction it caused

Key people

  • Senator Henry Dawes — Author of the Dawes Act — genuinely believed assimilation was in Native interests
  • President Grover Cleveland — Signed the Dawes Act into law — typical of "humanitarian" approach that destroyed Native culture

Key terms

  • Dawes Act — 1887 law — broke up tribal land into individual 160-acre allotments; forced assimilation
  • Allotment — Individual land parcel given to each Native American head of household
  • Assimilation — US policy goal — destroy Native American culture and absorb them into white American society
  • Surplus land — Reservation land left over after allotments — sold to white settlers; destroyed the reservation system

Key events

  • Dawes Act (1887) — At the time: presented as helping Natives by giving them individual property — Long term: destroyed collective tribal land ownership; lost 90 million acres

Key figures

  • 138 million acres — Native American land before Dawes Act — the reservation system
  • 48 million acres — Native American land by 1934 — lost ~90 million acres due to Dawes Act
  • 160 acres — Each Native American head of household received — compared to millions of collective tribal acres
  • 25 years — Period before allotted land could be sold — designed to "protect" Natives; in practice sold off rapidly

Battle of Wounded Knee (1890)

Key dates

  • 1889 — Sitting Bull killed by Indian police — US government feared Ghost Dance movement
  • 29 December 1890 — Wounded Knee Massacre — 7th Cavalry killed ~250–300 Lakota Sioux
  • 1890 — Symbolic end of the Indian Wars — final military suppression of Native resistance

Key people

  • Sitting Bull — Spiritual leader — killed by Indian police before Wounded Knee while being arrested
  • Big Foot — Lakota chief — led his band to Wounded Knee; killed in the massacre
  • Wovoka — Paiute prophet — founded the Ghost Dance movement; promised spiritual renewal

Key terms

  • Ghost Dance — Spiritual movement 1890 — Sioux believed it would restore buffalo and drive out white settlers
  • Wounded Knee — Site of the 1890 massacre — symbol of the destruction of Native American culture
  • 7th Cavalry — Regiment that carried out the massacre — same regiment Custer commanded at Little Bighorn

Key events

  • Slavery — The defining difference — economic foundation of Southern society
  • States' rights — Southern argument — states should decide on slavery, not federal government
  • Abolitionism — Movement to end slavery — stronger in the North

Key events

Attitudes to Natives - Humanitarians and Exterminators

Key dates

  • 1870s–1880s — Peak of the "exterminator" attitude — army campaigns and buffalo slaughter
  • 1879 — Carlisle Indian Industrial School founded — humanitarian assimilation in action
  • 1881 — Helen Hunt Jackson publishes A Century of Dishonour — humanitarian critique of US policy

Key people

  • General William Sherman — "Exterminator" — "the only good Indian is a dead Indian"; encouraged buffalo slaughter as policy
  • Richard Henry Pratt — "Humanitarian" — founded Carlisle School; wanted assimilation, not extermination
  • Helen Hunt Jackson — Genuine humanitarian — wrote A Century of Dishonour (1881) condemning broken treaties

Key terms

  • Humanitarian — Those who believed Natives should be "helped" through assimilation into white society — destroyed culture "kindly"
  • Exterminator — Those who favoured outright military destruction of Native peoples
  • Assimilation — Forcing Natives to adopt white American culture — through boarding schools, Dawes Act
  • Kill the Indian, save the man — Slogan of Richard Henry Pratt — captures the humanitarian assimilation attitude
  • Boarding schools — Institutions removing Native children from their families — forced assimilation

Key events

  • Carlisle Indian School (1879) — At the time: celebrated as "civilising" Natives — Long term: destroyed Native languages, culture, and family ties
  • Buffalo slaughter (1870s–80s) — At the time: economic activity and deliberate policy — Long term: destroyed the material basis of Plains Indian life

Key figures

  • ~600,000 — Native American population around 1800 — fell to ~237,000 by 1900

The Resolution of the Indian Problem - Overview

Key dates

  • 1870s–80s — Reservation system consolidated — Plains Indians confined to shrinking land
  • 1887 — Dawes Act — broke up communal tribal land into individual allotments
  • 1890 — Wounded Knee Massacre — end of armed Native American resistance
  • 1900 — Native American population at historic low — ~237,000

Key people

  • General William Sherman — Military architect of the "exterminator" approach — "kill the buffalo, kill the Indians"
  • Senator Henry Dawes — Legislative architect of assimilation — the Dawes Act bears his name
  • President Ulysses S Grant — "Peace Policy" — tried assimilation through reservations; still resulted in land loss and cultural destruction

Key terms

  • Indian Problem — US government's term for Native resistance to white expansion — framed as a problem to be solved
  • Reservation system — Confinement of Native Americans to defined parcels of land — replacing free-ranging life
  • Assimilation — Forced adoption of white American culture — boarding schools, Dawes Act, banning of ceremonies
  • Cultural genocide — Destruction of Native language, religion, and identity — the long-term result of US policy

Key events

  • Reservation system (1870s–80s) — At the time: confined Native Americans physically — Long term: destroyed the nomadic lifestyle and buffalo-based economy
  • Dawes Act (1887) — At the time: presented as giving Natives individual property rights — Long term: lost 90 million acres; broke up tribal communities
  • Wounded Knee (1890) — At the time: ended armed resistance — Long term: symbol of cultural destruction; Native Americans left in poverty on tiny reservations

Key figures

  • ~90 million acres — Native land lost under the Dawes Act — from 138 million to 48 million acres
  • ~237,000 — Native Americans remaining by 1900 — down from ~600,000 in 1800
