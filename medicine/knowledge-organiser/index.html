Overview & the big picture

Medicine Overview

Key dates

  • c.400 BC — Hippocrates — natural causes of disease; foundation of Western medicine
  • c.200 AD — Galen — built on Hippocrates; dominated medical thinking for ~1,400 years
  • 1348 — Black Death — exposed the total failure of medieval medicine
  • 1543 — Vesalius — overturned Galen's anatomy; a turning point
  • 1628 — Harvey — proved blood circulates; showed the scientific method could challenge authority
  • 1796 — Jenner — smallpox vaccination; first successful vaccine
  • 1861 — Germ Theory (Pasteur) — identified the true cause of disease
  • 1948 — NHS founded — universal healthcare for the first time

Key people

  • Hippocrates — c.460 BC — natural causes; clinical observation; Hippocratic Oath
  • Galen — c.200 AD — built on Hippocrates; adopted by Church; both helped and hindered medicine
  • Vesalius — 1543 — corrected Galen's anatomy; showed authority could be challenged
  • Pasteur — 1861 — Germ Theory; transformed understanding of the cause of disease
  • Fleming — 1928 — discovered penicillin; enabled the antibiotic revolution

Key terms

  • Four Humours — Blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile — Hippocratic theory; dominated medicine for centuries
  • Miasma theory — Disease caused by bad air — wrong, but drove some useful public health action
  • Germ Theory — Disease caused by microbes — Pasteur 1861; the key breakthrough in understanding disease
  • NHS — National Health Service — universal free healthcare from cradle to grave

Key events

  • Black Death (1348) — At the time: medicine utterly helpless — Long term: exposed the limits of Galenic theory
  • Germ Theory (1861) — At the time: transformed understanding of disease — Long term: enabled vaccines, antibiotics, and modern medicine
  • NHS (1948) — At the time: radical state intervention in healthcare — Long term: transformed life expectancy and access to medicine

Key figures

  • ~1,400 years — Period Galen's ideas dominated without challenge — illustrates the power of authority
  • 1/3 — Proportion of England's population killed by the Black Death (1348)
  • ~400,000 — Europeans dying from smallpox per year before Jenner's vaccine

BPSF - Medicine Stands Still

Key dates

  • c.200 AD — Galen writes — his ideas then dominate for the next 1,400 years
  • 476 AD — Fall of Rome — loss of Roman public health infrastructure; regression in practical medicine
  • c.1000–1350 — Height of medieval medicine — Galenic theory unchanged; Church controls learning

Key people

  • Galen — c.200 AD — his dominance is what "standing still" means; wrong in many areas but untouchable
  • Medieval Church — Controlled universities and medicine — reinforced Galen as unquestionable authority

Key terms

  • Four Humours — Blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile — the dominant explanation of disease
  • Miasma theory — Bad air causes disease — wrong, but not challenged in this period
  • Bloodletting — Removing blood to rebalance the humours — harmful treatment used for centuries
  • Church authority — The Church backed Galen — questioning him meant questioning God

Key events

  • Church adopts Galen (medieval period) — At the time: gave authority to medical ideas — Long term: prevented new ideas from being accepted
  • Fall of Rome (476 AD) — At the time: loss of Roman public health systems — Long term: towns became dirtier; disease spread more easily

Key figures

  • ~1,400 years — Galen's ideas went largely unchallenged (c.200 AD to c.1600 AD)
  • ~90% — Medieval hospitals were Church-run — religion, not science, shaped medical care

BPSF - Beginnings of Change

Key dates

  • c.1440 — Gutenberg's printing press — transformed the spread of ideas across Europe
  • 1453 — Fall of Constantinople — Greek scholars fled west, bringing ancient texts
  • 1543 — Vesalius publishes De Humani Corporis Fabrica — corrected over 200 Galenic errors
  • 1628 — Harvey proves blood circulates — challenged Galen; showed experiment over authority

Key people

  • Vesalius — 1543 — overturned Galen through dissection; did not explain why things worked, but what they look like
  • William Harvey — 1628 — proved blood circulates; used measurement and experiment
  • Ambroise Paré — Renaissance surgeon — improved wound treatment through observation and trial

Key terms

  • Renaissance — "Rebirth" of classical learning — questioning of authority; new attitudes to knowledge
  • Printing press — Gutenberg c.1440 — enabled rapid spread of new ideas across Europe
  • Humanism — Renaissance philosophy — humans, not just God, can investigate and understand the world
  • Scientific method — Observe, experiment, conclude — the new approach replacing Galen's authority

Key events

  • Printing press (c.1440) — At the time: allowed books to spread fast — Long term: new medical ideas could reach a European audience
  • Vesalius publishes (1543) — At the time: challenged Galen's anatomy — Long term: transformed how doctors understood the body
  • Harvey's circulation (1628) — At the time: overturned Galen's blood theory — Long term: showed experiment could defeat authority

Key figures

  • 200+ — Errors in Galen's anatomy corrected by Vesalius — based on actual human dissection
  • 20 million — Books in circulation within 50 years of the printing press — speed of knowledge spread

BPSF - A Revolution in Medicine

Key dates

  • 1796 — Jenner — smallpox vaccination; first successful vaccine
  • 1847 — Simpson — chloroform anaesthetic; allowed longer, more complex surgery
  • 1861 — Pasteur — Germ Theory published; the key breakthrough
  • 1865 — Lister — carbolic acid antiseptic; dramatically cut surgical death rates
  • 1882 — Koch identifies TB bacterium — Germ Theory put to use

Key people

  • Louis Pasteur — 1861 — Germ Theory; the single most important breakthrough in this period
  • Robert Koch — Identified specific bacteria causing specific diseases — turned theory into practice
  • Joseph Lister — Applied Germ Theory to surgery — antiseptic technique
  • James Simpson — Chloroform anaesthetic 1847 — removed pain; enabled more complex operations

Key terms

  • Germ Theory — Disease caused by microbes — Pasteur 1861; foundation of modern medicine
  • Anaesthetic — Substance allowing painless surgery — chloroform (Simpson 1847)
  • Antiseptic — Kills bacteria to prevent infection — carbolic acid (Lister 1865)
  • Vaccination — Using a weakened form of a disease to build immunity

Key events

  • Pasteur's Germ Theory (1861) — At the time: explained how disease spreads — Long term: enabled vaccines, antibiotics, antiseptics
  • Lister's antiseptics (1865) — At the time: dramatically cut surgical deaths — Long term: made complex surgery survivable

Key figures

  • ~50% — Operations ended in death from infection before antiseptics
  • 15% — Death rate after surgery dropped to ~15% after Lister's antiseptics
  • 400,000+ — Europeans dying of smallpox per year before Jenner

BPSF - Modern Medicine

Key dates

  • 1909 — Ehrlich — Salvarsan 606; first "magic bullet" chemical treatment
  • 1928 — Fleming discovers penicillin — the antibiotic revolution begins
  • 1942 — Beveridge Report — blueprint for the welfare state
  • 1948 — NHS founded — universal free healthcare
  • 1953 — Watson and Crick — DNA double helix; foundation of genetic medicine

Key people

  • Alexander Fleming — 1928 — discovered penicillin by accident; illustrates chance + prepared mind
  • Aneurin Bevan — Architect of the NHS — fought resistance from doctors to establish universal healthcare
  • Watson and Crick — 1953 — DNA double helix; built on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray data

Key terms

  • Penicillin — First antibiotic — Fleming 1928, developed by Florey and Chain
  • NHS — National Health Service — free healthcare from cradle to grave from 1948
  • DNA — Deoxyribonucleic acid — Watson and Crick 1953; transformed understanding of heredity and disease
  • Magic bullet — A chemical that targets a specific disease without harming the body

Key events

  • Penicillin (1928–43) — At the time: accidental discovery — Long term: transformed treatment of infection; saved millions in WW2
  • NHS (1948) — At the time: radical universal healthcare — Long term: transformed British life expectancy and access to medicine
  • DNA discovery (1953) — At the time: revealed the structure of life — Long term: enabled genetic engineering, gene therapy, personalised medicine

Key figures

  • £437 million — Cost of NHS in its first year — far more than predicted; showed scale of unmet need
  • 200 million — Estimated lives saved by penicillin since its development
  • 3 billion — Base pairs in the human genome — scale of the genetic revolution

Medieval medicine, c1000–1500

Importance of Hippocrates

Key dates

  • c.460 BC — Hippocrates born — shifted medicine from supernatural to natural explanations
  • c.400 BC — Hippocratic Corpus compiled — ~60 texts; the foundation of Western medical theory
  • c.200 AD — Galen builds on Hippocrates — extended his ideas; kept them dominant for centuries more

Key people

  • Hippocrates — ~460 BC — "Father of Medicine"; natural causes; clinical observation; Hippocratic Oath
  • Galen — c.200 AD — extended Hippocratic ideas; Four Humours survived through his influence

Key terms

  • Four Humours — Blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile — Hippocratic theory of disease; balance = health
  • Clinical observation — Watching and recording symptoms — Hippocrates' key method; still used today
  • Hippocratic Oath — Code of medical ethics — doctors still swear a version today
  • Natural causes — Disease caused by nature, not gods — the central breakthrough of Hippocratic medicine

Key events

  • Hippocratic Corpus compiled (~400 BC) — At the time: first rational framework for medicine — Long term: adopted by Galen, then the Church; dominated for 1,400 years

Key figures

  • ~60 — Hippocratic texts that survived — a library of clinical observation
  • ~400 — Treatments recorded in the Hippocratic Corpus

The Importance of Galen and Doctor Training

Key dates

  • c.460 BC — Hippocrates — natural causes; Galen builds on these foundations
  • c.200 AD — Galen active — writes anatomy and medical theory based on animal dissection
  • 1543 — Vesalius — exposes Galen's anatomical errors; begins the overturning of his authority
  • 1876 — Koch identifies anthrax — Germ Theory finally displaces Galenic humour theory

Key people

  • Hippocrates — Foundation for Galen — his natural causes and Four Humours framework
  • Galen — c.200 AD — extended Hippocrates; Church adopted his work; wrong about anatomy but right to use observation
  • Vesalius — 1543 — showed Galen wrong through human dissection; key challenge to authority
  • Robert Koch — 1876 — Germ Theory finally replaced humour theory as the explanation of disease

Key terms

  • Vivisection — Dissection of living animals — Galen's method; led to anatomical errors
  • Four Humours — Galen's extension of Hippocrates — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile
  • Pig experiment — Galen cut vocal cords of a live pig to show the brain controls the voice — impressive but also shows limits of animal work
  • Church authority — The Church endorsed Galen — questioning him was near-heresy

Key events

  • Galen's pig experiment — At the time: demonstrated the brain controls speech — Long term: made Galen's methods seem credible; reinforced his authority
  • Church adopts Galen (medieval period) — At the time: stabilised medical education — Long term: prevented challenge to Galen for centuries
  • Vesalius corrects Galen (1543) — At the time: shocking overturning of authority — Long term: opened the door to scientific anatomy

Key figures

  • 200+ — Errors in Galen's anatomy (based on animal dissection, not human)
  • ~1,400 years — Galen's ideas dominated medicine after the fall of Rome until the Renaissance

Impact of the Christian Church on Medicine in the Middle Ages

Key dates

  • 476 AD — Fall of Rome — Church becomes the dominant institution in Western Europe
  • 1130 — Council of Clermont — Church restricted clergy from performing surgery
  • c.1000–1350 — Height of Church influence on medicine — Galen's works taught in Church universities

Key people

  • Medieval Church — Controlled universities and hospitals — both helped (care of the sick) and hindered (blocked new ideas) medicine
  • Thomas Aquinas — 13th C — reconciled Christian faith with classical learning; kept Galen's ideas intellectually respectable

Key terms

  • Church authority — The Church backed Galen's ideas as consistent with Christian thinking — challenged = heresy
  • Dissection ban — Church restricted human dissection — slowed anatomical knowledge
  • Prayer as medicine — The Church taught illness was God's punishment — prayer and pilgrimage were treatments
  • Pilgrimage — Journey to holy sites for healing — part of the Church's approach to medicine

Key events

  • Church adopts Galen (medieval) — At the time: gave stability to medical education — Long term: prevented anatomical progress for centuries
  • Council of Clermont (1130) — At the time: banned clergy from surgery — Long term: separated surgery from learned medicine; barber-surgeons emerged

Key figures

  • ~90% — Medieval European hospitals were Church-run — religion shaped all medical care

Medicine in the Islamic World Compared with Europe

Key dates

  • c.800–1200 AD — Islamic Golden Age of medicine — while Europe stagnated, Islamic scholars advanced
  • c.900s — Al-Razi writing — identified measles and smallpox as separate diseases
  • c.1025 — Avicenna's Canon of Medicine published — the most comprehensive medical textbook of the medieval world

Key people

  • Ibn Sina (Avicenna) — c.980–1037 — Canon of Medicine; the most important Islamic medical scholar
  • Al-Razi (Rhazes) — c.854–925 — distinguished measles from smallpox; ran the Baghdad hospital
  • Al-Zahrawi — 10th C — wrote a surgical encyclopaedia; pioneered many surgical tools

Key terms

  • Islamic Golden Age — c.800–1200 AD — Islamic scholars translated, preserved and extended Greek medical texts
  • Canon of Medicine — Ibn Sina (Avicenna) c.1025 — encyclopaedic medical text; adopted by European universities
  • Bimaristan — Islamic hospital — more advanced than European equivalents; separate wards for different diseases
  • Al-Razi (Rhazes) — Identified measles and smallpox as distinct diseases — practical clinical observation

Key events

  • Translation movement (8th–9th C) — At the time: Islamic scholars preserved and extended Greek texts — Long term: these texts returned to Europe and helped spark the Renaissance
  • Canon of Medicine (1025) — At the time: the most advanced medical textbook — Long term: used in European universities until the 17th century

Key figures

  • 5 volumes — Avicenna's Canon of Medicine — covered all known medicine; used in European universities for 600 years

Public Health - Towns and Monasteries

Key dates

  • Pre-476 AD — Roman public health — aqueducts, sewers, public baths; lost after fall of Rome
  • c.1000–1350 — Medieval town growth — overcrowding, poor sanitation, disease
  • c.1000–1350 — Monasteries — relatively clean, organised; contrast with town conditions

Key people

  • Medieval monks — Ran hospitals and herb gardens — the most advanced healthcare providers in medieval Europe
  • Town authorities — Occasionally cleared waste under miasma theory — right action, wrong reason

Key terms

  • Miasma theory — Bad air causes disease — explains why authorities sometimes cleaned streets (for wrong reasons)
  • Monastery — Religious community — better sanitation than towns; running water, drains, herb gardens
  • Quarantine — Isolation of the sick — an early public health measure; used for leprosy
  • Leper house — Isolation hospital for those with leprosy — shows some understanding of contagion

Key events

  • Fall of Rome (476 AD) — At the time: collapse of Roman public health infrastructure — Long term: towns became far dirtier; disease spread more easily
  • Medieval town growth — At the time: overcrowded towns with no sanitation — Long term: created ideal conditions for epidemic disease like the Black Death

Key figures

  • ~30,000–80,000 — Population of medieval London — densely packed, minimal sanitation

Surgery in the Middle Ages

Key dates

  • c.1163 — Council of Tours — Church banned clergy from performing surgery
  • c.1000–1350 — Medieval surgery period — limited by lack of anaesthesia, antisepsis, and anatomy
  • 1536 — Paré's battlefield discovery — accident that began to improve surgical practice

Key people

  • Barber-surgeons — Performed amputations, tooth extractions, bloodletting — practical but low status
  • Hugh of Lucca — 13th C Italian surgeon — challenged cauterisation, used wine to clean wounds
  • Ambroise Paré — 16th C — improved wound treatment; ligature instead of cauterisation

Key terms

  • Barber-surgeon — Performed surgery but not trained physicians — socially lower status than doctors
  • Cauterisation — Sealing wounds with burning hot iron — painful, caused shock; standard practice
  • Trepanning — Drilling a hole in the skull — one of the oldest surgical procedures
  • Bloodletting — Removing blood to rebalance humours — widespread but harmful

Key events

  • Council of Tours (1163) — At the time: banned clergy from surgery — Long term: separated surgery from medicine; created the lowly barber-surgeon
  • Crusades (1095–1291) — At the time: battlefield surgery developed — Long term: some practical surgical knowledge gained from treating wounds

Key figures

  • ~50% — Proportion of surgical patients who died from infection or shock
  • Under 2 minutes — Time a skilled amputator could remove a limb — speed was survival (no anaesthetic)

Black Death (1348-49)

Key dates

  • 1347 — Black Death arrives in Sicily from Asia — spreads rapidly through Europe
  • 1348–49 — Peak in England — kills roughly 1/3 of the population
  • 1350s onwards — Returns repeatedly — medieval Europe never fully recovered demographically

Key people

  • Medieval physicians — Helpless — prescribed herbs, bloodletting, prayer; nothing worked
  • Edward III — King during the Black Death — government response was ineffective; attempted some quarantine measures

Key terms

  • Black Death — Bubonic plague caused by Yersinia pestis — spread by fleas on rats
  • Miasma theory — Dominant explanation — bad air causes disease; explains ineffective responses
  • Flagellants — People who whipped themselves to appease God — shows the religious response to disease
  • Quarantine — Isolation of the sick — one of the few partially effective responses

Key events

  • Black Death in England (1348) — At the time: medicine completely powerless — Long term: exposed the total failure of humour theory; may have stimulated questioning of authority
  • Flagellant movement — At the time: religious response to pestilence — Long term: shows how far medicine failed; people turned to religion

Key figures

  • 1/3 — Proportion of England's population killed — approximately 1.5–2 million people
  • 25 million — Deaths across Europe from the Black Death
  • Up to 60% — Death rates in some English towns — shows the total failure of medical response

The Medical Renaissance, c1500–1700

Beginnings of Change

Key dates

  • c.1440 — Gutenberg printing press — allowed new ideas to spread rapidly across Europe
  • 1453 — Fall of Constantinople — Greek scholars fled west; brought ancient texts with them
  • 1543 — Vesalius publishes — the clearest single example of change beginning

Key people

  • Gutenberg — Printing press c.1440 — indirect but fundamental factor in enabling medical change
  • Vesalius — 1543 — most clear example of new Renaissance approach; challenged Galen through direct observation

Key terms

  • Renaissance — "Rebirth" — new willingness to question classical authority including Galen
  • Printing press — Key factor enabling change — new medical ideas could reach a wide audience
  • Humanism — Renaissance philosophy — humans can investigate and understand the world
  • Scientific method — Observation and experiment replacing reliance on ancient authority

Key events

  • Printing press (c.1440) — At the time: revolutionary spread of knowledge — Long term: enabled Vesalius, Harvey and others to reach a European audience
  • Fall of Constantinople (1453) — At the time: Greek scholars fled to Western Europe — Long term: brought ancient Greek and Arabic medical texts; stimulated new thinking

Key figures

  • 20 million — Books circulating in Europe within 50 years of the printing press
  • 200+ — Errors in Galen corrected by Vesalius — enabled by human dissection, not animal

Vesalius (1543)

Key dates

  • 1514 — Vesalius born in Brussels — trained at Paris and Padua universities
  • 1543 — De Humani Corporis Fabrica published — same year as Copernicus's heliocentric model
  • 1543 onwards — Vesalius's work gradually replaces Galen in medical education

Key people

  • Andreas Vesalius — 1514–64 — overturned Galen's anatomy through direct human dissection; key Renaissance figure
  • Galen — c.200 AD — his animal-based anatomy shown to be wrong by Vesalius
  • Emperor Charles V — Holy Roman Emperor — patronised Vesalius; shows the role of individuals in enabling change

Key terms

  • De Humani Corporis Fabrica — "On the Structure of the Human Body" 1543 — Vesalius's landmark anatomical work
  • Anatomy — Study of the structure of the body — Vesalius based his on human dissection
  • Dissection — Cutting open bodies to observe anatomy — Vesalius obtained bodies of executed criminals

Key events

  • Publication of De Humani Corporis Fabrica (1543) — At the time: directly challenged Galen's authority — Long term: transformed anatomy; proved observation over authority

Key figures

  • 200+ — Errors in Galen's anatomy identified by Vesalius — all from dissecting animals, not humans
  • 7 volumes — De Humani Corporis Fabrica — with detailed woodcut illustrations of the human body

Paré

Key dates

  • 1536 — Battle of Villane — Paré runs out of boiling oil; accidentally discovers gentler treatment
  • 1545 — Paré publishes his Method of Treating Wounds — sharing his discoveries
  • 1561 — Paré publishes work on surgery including use of ligatures

Key people

  • Ambroise Paré — 1510–90 — French battlefield surgeon; replaced boiling oil with egg yolk mixture; used ligatures; designed prosthetics
  • Vesalius — Contemporary — both show how Renaissance observation over authority transformed medicine

Key terms

  • Ligature — Tying off blood vessels instead of cauterisation — Paré's key surgical innovation
  • Cauterisation — Burning wounds with hot iron or boiling oil — the standard (painful, shocking) practice before Paré
  • Battlefield surgery — Context of Paré's discoveries — war drove practical innovation
  • Prosthetics — Paré also designed artificial limbs — shows breadth of his contribution

Key events

  • Battle of Villane (1536) — At the time: accidental discovery that gentler wound treatment worked better — Long term: began to replace cauterisation; an example of chance leading to progress
  • Paré publishes (1545, 1561) — At the time: shared new surgical methods — Long term: printing press enabled his ideas to reach other surgeons

Key figures

  • 3 — Paré's egg yolk, rose oil and turpentine mixture components — replaced boiling oil

Harvey (1628)

Key dates

  • 1578 — Harvey born — trained at Padua (Italy), the leading anatomy university
  • 1628 — De Motu Cordis published — proved blood circulates continuously

Key people

  • William Harvey — 1578–1657 — proved blood circulates; used measurement and experiment; royal physician to James I and Charles I
  • Galen — c.200 AD — believed blood was made in the liver and consumed; Harvey proved this wrong
  • Charles I — King of England — Harvey was his physician; royal patronage enabled his research

Key terms

  • De Motu Cordis — "On the Motion of the Heart and Blood" 1628 — Harvey's published proof of blood circulation
  • Blood circulation — Blood pumped by the heart, circulates continuously — overturned Galen's theory that blood was made in the liver
  • Hypothesis and experiment — Harvey's method — formed a theory, then tested it; a model of the scientific method

Key events

  • Publication of De Motu Cordis (1628) — At the time: overturned Galen's blood theory — Long term: demonstrated the power of experiment; directly contradicted the Church-endorsed Galen

Key figures

  • ~200 litres — Amount of blood Harvey calculated the heart pumps per hour — proved blood must circulate

Great Plague vs the Black Death (1665)

Key dates

  • 1348–49 — Black Death — roughly 1/3 of England died; medicine totally helpless
  • 1665 — Great Plague of London — ~100,000 died; same helplessness despite 300+ years passing
  • 1666 — Great Fire of London — widely thought to have ended the plague; coincidence, not causation

Key people

  • Samuel Pepys — Diarist — recorded the Great Plague vividly; shows contemporary responses
  • Charles II — King during the Great Plague — fled London; government largely ineffective

Key terms

  • Plague doctor — Wore a beak-mask filled with herbs — protecting against "miasma" (bad air)
  • Miasma theory — Still the dominant explanation in 1665 — same wrong theory as 1348
  • Bills of Mortality — Weekly death records in London — early public health data collection
  • Quarantine — Sealing infected houses with red crosses — one of the few effective measures

Key events

  • Great Plague (1665) — At the time: same responses as 1348 — Long term: shows the limits of change; Germ Theory still 200 years away
  • Great Fire of London (1666) — At the time: destroyed much of the city — Long term: coincided with end of plague; often wrongly credited

Key figures

  • 1/3 — Population of England killed in Black Death 1348–49
  • ~100,000 — Deaths in the Great Plague of London 1665 — about 1/4 of London's population
  • ~315 years — Between the two plagues — almost no improvement in understanding or treatment

John Hunter

Key dates

  • 1728 — John Hunter born in Scotland — became the leading surgeon/anatomist of the 18th century
  • 1759 — Hunter opens anatomy school in London
  • 1783 — Hunter becomes surgeon to King George III

Key people

  • John Hunter — 1728–93 — the father of scientific surgery; experimental approach; trained Jenner
  • Edward Jenner — Hunter's student — vaccination for smallpox (1796); shows the impact of Hunter's mentoring

Key terms

  • Experimental surgery — Hunter's approach — test ideas through experiment, not just follow tradition
  • Scientific method — Hunter applied it to surgery — observe, experiment, conclude
  • Anatomy school — Hunter's school trained many leading surgeons including Edward Jenner

Key events

  • Hunter opens anatomy school (1759) — At the time: trained a generation of surgeons — Long term: spread the scientific approach to surgery
  • Hunter's anatomical museum — At the time: largest collection of anatomical specimens — Long term: became the Hunterian Collection at the Royal College of Surgeons

Key figures

  • ~500 — Animals dissected by Hunter in his anatomical studies
  • 13,000+ — Specimens in Hunter's anatomical museum — the Hunterian Collection

Quacks, Sydenham and Impact of the Renaissance

Key dates

  • 1624 — Thomas Sydenham born — developed clinical observation as a medical method
  • 1666 — Sydenham publishes on epidemic diseases — the "English Hippocrates"
  • 17th–18th C — Quack medicine widespread — shows the limits of real medical progress

Key people

  • Thomas Sydenham — 1624–89 — "English Hippocrates"; rejected Galenic theory; used careful clinical observation; classified diseases
  • Various quacks — Exploited public fear — show the gap between elite medicine and everyday healthcare

Key terms

  • Quack — Untrained or fraudulent healer — sold ineffective remedies for profit
  • Thomas Sydenham — "English Hippocrates" — clinical observation; classified diseases by symptom
  • Clinical observation — Sydenham's method — observe the patient, record symptoms, identify patterns
  • Alchemy — Pseudo-science mixing with medicine — showed the confusion of the period

Key events

  • Sydenham's clinical work (1660s–80s) — At the time: returned to Hippocratic observation — Long term: influenced Enlightenment medicine; clinical observation became standard
  • Quack medicine (17th–18th C) — At the time: widespread — Long term: shows that despite scientific progress, unproven remedies remained popular

Revolution in medicine, c1700–1900

Nursing and Changes to Hospitals (18th-19th C)

Key dates

  • 1854–56 — Crimean War — Nightingale at Scutari Hospital; transformed military nursing
  • 1859 — Nightingale publishes Notes on Nursing
  • 1863 — Red Cross founded by Henri Dunant — nursing as a humanitarian profession

  • Florence Nightingale — 1820–1910 — professionalised nursing; used statistics to prove sanitation saves lives
  • Mary Seacole — 1805–81 — Crimea nurse; her contribution was overlooked; recognition came much later

Key terms

  • Florence Nightingale — "Lady with the Lamp" — transformed nursing into a professional, respected occupation
  • Nursing — Before Nightingale: low-status, disorganised — After: professional, trained

  • Mary Seacole — Black Jamaican nurse in Crimea — provided care closer to the front; largely unrecognised at the time

Key events

  • Nightingale at Scutari (1854) — At the time: transformed nursing and hospital sanitation — Long term: created the modern nursing profession
  • Publication of Notes on Nursing (1859) — At the time: practical nursing guide — Long term: spread Nightingale's approach globally

  • 42% — Death rate at Scutari Hospital before Nightingale arrived
  • 2% — Death rate at Scutari Hospital after Nightingale's reforms — a dramatic reduction
  • ~4,000 — Lives Nightingale estimated she saved through improved sanitation and nursing

Edward Jenner and Vaccination (1796)

Key dates

  • 1796 — Jenner tests cowpox vaccine on James Phipps — the first vaccination
  • 1798 — Jenner publishes his findings — vaccination goes public
  • 1840 — Vaccination made free by British government
  • 1853 — Vaccination made compulsory in Britain
  • 1980 — WHO declares smallpox eradicated — Jenner's vaccine achieved this

Key people

  • Edward Jenner — 1749–1823 — trained by John Hunter; tested observation with experiment; vaccination pioneer
  • James Phipps — Jenner's test subject — 8-year-old boy vaccinated 1796; survived and was protected

Key terms

  • Vaccination — Using a weakened or related disease to create immunity — Jenner's term (from vacca, cow)
  • Cowpox — Mild disease in dairy maids — Jenner noticed they didn't catch smallpox
  • Variolation — The dangerous predecessor — deliberately infecting with live smallpox

Key events

  • Jenner vaccinates James Phipps (1796) — At the time: first vaccine; tested on a child (ethically problematic by today's standards) — Long term: eradicated smallpox; founded the principle of vaccination
  • Government funds vaccination (1840) — At the time: first government-funded medical intervention — Long term: established the principle of state involvement in public health

Key figures

  • ~400,000 — Europeans dying from smallpox each year before Jenner
  • 1980 — Year smallpox was officially eradicated — the only human disease fully eliminated

Anaesthetics

Key dates

  • 1842 — Ether first used in surgery (USA) — Crawford Long
  • 1846 — Ether demonstrated publicly in Boston — news spreads to Britain
  • 1847 — James Simpson uses chloroform — easier to administer than ether
  • 1853 — Queen Victoria uses chloroform during childbirth — gave it social acceptability

Key people

  • James Simpson — 1811–70 — pioneered use of chloroform; faced religious opposition ("pain is God's will")
  • Queen Victoria — Used chloroform 1853 — royal endorsement defeated much of the religious objection

Key terms

  • Anaesthetic — Substance that removes pain during surgery — transformed what surgery was possible
  • Ether — First widely used anaesthetic — effective but irritating and flammable
  • Chloroform — Simpson's anaesthetic 1847 — easier to use than ether; became standard

Key events

  • Simpson uses chloroform (1847) — At the time: removed pain from surgery — Long term: allowed longer, more complex operations; but also a "Black Period" (more operations, still high infection deaths)
  • Queen Victoria uses chloroform (1853) — At the time: royal endorsement — Long term: silenced religious critics; made anaesthetics respectable

Key figures

  • Under 2 minutes — Time a skilled surgeon could amputate a leg — speed was everything before anaesthetics
  • ~1/3 — Proportion of patients dying from surgical shock before anaesthetics

Anaesthetics and Antiseptics

Key dates

  • 1847 — Simpson — chloroform anaesthetic; longer operations now possible
  • 1865 — Lister first uses carbolic acid as antiseptic in surgery
  • 1867 — Lister publishes his findings on antiseptic technique
  • 1890s — Aseptic technique replaces antiseptic — creating a sterile environment

Key people

  • Joseph Lister — 1827–1912 — developed antiseptic surgery; directly inspired by Pasteur's Germ Theory
  • Louis Pasteur — Germ Theory 1861 — inspired Lister; shows the link between scientific theory and practical medicine
  • James Simpson — 1847 — anaesthetics that enabled longer surgery; but created the "Black Period" before antiseptics caught up

Key terms

  • Antiseptic — Kills bacteria on wounds and instruments — carbolic acid (Lister 1865)
  • Aseptic — Creating a sterile environment to prevent bacteria entering — the next step beyond antiseptic
  • Carbolic acid — Lister's antiseptic — sprayed on wounds, instruments and surgeons' hands
  • Black Period — The period when anaesthetics existed but antiseptics didn't — more operations, more deaths from infection

Key events

  • Lister uses carbolic acid (1865) — At the time: dramatic cut in surgical deaths — Long term: made surgery survivable; built on Pasteur's Germ Theory
  • Aseptic surgery (1890s) — At the time: further reduced infection — Long term: enabled the full range of modern surgery

Key figures

  • ~50% — Operations resulting in death from infection before Lister
  • ~15% — Surgical death rate after Lister's antiseptics — a dramatic improvement

Germ Theory

Key dates

  • 1857 — Pasteur begins fermentation research — microbes cause decay, not spontaneous generation
  • 1861 — Pasteur publishes Germ Theory — microbes cause disease
  • 1865 — Lister applies Germ Theory to surgery — antiseptics
  • 1876 — Koch identifies anthrax bacterium — Germ Theory put to practical use

Key people

  • Louis Pasteur — 1822–95 — French chemist; Germ Theory 1861; vaccines for cholera and anthrax
  • Robert Koch — 1843–1910 — German rival; identified specific bacteria causing specific diseases
  • Joseph Lister — Applied Pasteur's Germ Theory to surgery — antiseptic technique (1865)

Key terms

  • Germ Theory — Microbes (germs) cause disease — Pasteur 1861; the foundational breakthrough
  • Spontaneous generation — Old theory — life forms spontaneously from non-living matter; Pasteur disproved this
  • Microbe — Microscopic organism — bacteria, viruses; the cause of infectious disease
  • Swan-neck flask experiment — Pasteur's proof — broth went bad only when exposed to air-borne microbes

Key events

  • Pasteur's Germ Theory (1861) — At the time: revolutionary explanation of disease — Long term: enabled antiseptics, vaccines, antibiotics; the single most important discovery in medicine
  • Koch identifies anthrax (1876) — At the time: proved a specific germ caused a specific disease — Long term: opened the era of "microbe hunting"

Koch and Microbe Hunting

Key dates

  • 1876 — Koch identifies anthrax bacillus — first specific bacterium linked to a specific disease
  • 1882 — Koch identifies the tuberculosis (TB) bacterium
  • 1883 — Koch identifies the cholera bacterium
  • 1909 — Ehrlich's Salvarsan 606 — magic bullet for syphilis, built on Koch's techniques

Key people

  • Robert Koch — 1843–1910 — "microbe hunter"; developed staining and agar; identified anthrax, TB, cholera bacteria
  • Paul Ehrlich — Koch's student — developed Salvarsan 606 (1909); first magic bullet; showed Germ Theory could lead to cures
  • Louis Pasteur — Koch's rival — Germ Theory; competed with Koch on vaccines

Key terms

  • Koch's Postulates — Rules for proving a specific germ causes a specific disease — scientific method applied to bacteriology
  • Staining technique — Koch used dyes to make bacteria visible under a microscope — key technical innovation
  • Agar plates — Solid growth medium for bacteria — Koch's innovation; allowed isolation of specific bacteria
  • Bacterium — Single-celled microorganism — Koch identified which ones caused which diseases

Key events

  • Koch identifies TB (1882) — At the time: identified the cause of the biggest killer in Europe — Long term: enabled targeted treatment; eventually a vaccine
  • Koch identifies cholera (1883) — At the time: confirmed cholera was water-borne — Long term: vindicated John Snow's earlier work

Key figures

  • 1/7 — Europeans killed by TB when Koch identified its cause in 1882 — shows scale of the problem
  • 605 — Failed attempts before Ehrlich succeeded with Salvarsan 606

Pasteur - Vaccination and Magic Bullets

Key dates

  • 1879 — Pasteur discovers weakened chicken cholera gives immunity — new approach to vaccination
  • 1881 — Pasteur's public anthrax vaccine test at Pouilly-le-Fort — spectacular success
  • 1885 — Pasteur's rabies vaccine — saved 9-year-old Joseph Meister
  • 1909 — Ehrlich — Salvarsan 606; first magic bullet; chemical treatment for syphilis

Key people

  • Louis Pasteur — 1822–95 — developed vaccines for chicken cholera, anthrax, and rabies; extended Jenner's principle
  • Paul Ehrlich — 1854–1915 — Koch's student; developed Salvarsan 606; proved chemicals could target specific diseases
  • Robert Koch — Pasteur's rival — competition between them accelerated discovery

Key terms

  • Magic bullet — A chemical that targets and kills a specific germ without harming the body
  • Salvarsan 606 — Ehrlich's 1909 treatment for syphilis — the first synthetic drug; the first magic bullet
  • Attenuated vaccine — Using a weakened form of a germ to create immunity — Pasteur extended Jenner's principle

Key events

  • Pasteur's anthrax test (1881) — At the time: public proof of vaccination — Long term: transformed public and government confidence in vaccines
  • Salvarsan 606 (1909) — At the time: cured syphilis (a major killer) — Long term: launched the era of synthetic chemical medicine; precursor to antibiotics

Key figures

  • 605 — Failed attempts before Ehrlich's Salvarsan 606 succeeded
  • 25 — Sheep vaccinated with Pasteur's anthrax vaccine all survived; 25 unvaccinated all died

Why Were Living Conditions in the 1800s So Poor (Public Health)

  • 1800s — Industrial Revolution — rapid urbanisation creating terrible living conditions
  • 1842 — Chadwick's Sanitary Report — documented the appalling conditions of the working class
  • 1848 — First cholera epidemic in Britain — 53,000 deaths

Key people

  • Edwin Chadwick — 1800–90 — sanitary reformer; his report shocked the public; believed in miasma theory but recommended the right actions
  • Seebohm Rowntree — Later surveys of poverty — built on Chadwick's tradition; influenced Liberal Reforms

Key terms

  • Urbanisation — Rapid growth of industrial towns — overcrowding, poor sanitation, disease
  • Miasma theory — Still believed in the 1840s — but did drive some sanitation improvements
  • Sanitation — Provision of clean water and sewage disposal — the key public health need

Key events

  • Industrial Revolution (1800s) — At the time: rapid growth of industrial towns — Long term: created public health crisis; forced eventual government action
  • Chadwick's Sanitary Report (1842) — At the time: documented appalling conditions — Long term: eventually led to Public Health Acts

Key figures

Public Health Acts

Key dates

  • 1848 — First Public Health Act — voluntary; local councils could act but weren't forced to
  • 1875 — Second Public Health Act — compulsory; councils must provide clean water and sewers

Key people

  • Edwin Chadwick — His 1842 report led directly to the 1848 Act
  • Benjamin Disraeli — Conservative Prime Minister — passed the 1875 Act; showed Conservatives could support public health

  • Public Health Act — Law requiring local councils to improve sanitation — 1848 (voluntary) vs 1875 (compulsory)
  • Laissez-faire — Government reluctance to interfere — explains why 1848 Act was voluntary
  • Compulsory — The key word in the 1875 Act — finally forced councils to act
  • Local government — Councils responsible for public health — the mechanism of the Acts

  • Public Health Act 1848 — At the time: first government acknowledgement of responsibility — Long term: largely ineffective because voluntary
  • Public Health Act 1875 — At the time: compulsory sanitation for the first time — Long term: transformed life expectancy; a shift away from laissez-faire

Snow, Cholera and the Great Stink (1854/1858)

Key dates

  • 1831 — First cholera epidemic in Britain — ~53,000 deaths; total panic
  • 1854 — Broad Street pump outbreak — Snow maps the deaths and identifies the pump
  • 1858 — Great Stink — Thames so polluted Parliament couldn't function; forced action
  • 1859–65 — Bazalgette builds London's sewers — direct result of the Great Stink

Key people

  • John Snow — 1813–58 — epidemiologist; mapped cholera; removed the Broad Street pump handle; proved water transmission
  • Joseph Bazalgette — 1819–91 — engineer who built London's sewer system; his work saved more lives than any Victorian doctor

Key terms

  • Cholera — Waterborne disease — spread through contaminated water, not bad air
  • John Snow — 1813–58 — mapped cholera deaths; proved it was waterborne; the father of epidemiology
  • Broad Street pump — The contaminated water pump Snow identified as the source of the 1854 outbreak
  • Great Stink — Summer 1858 — Thames stench so bad Parliament recessed; forced sewer construction
  • Epidemiology — Study of disease patterns in populations — Snow founded this field

Key events

  • Broad Street pump outbreak (1854) — At the time: Snow identified water, not air, as the source — Long term: proved miasma theory wrong; a key step toward Germ Theory
  • Great Stink (1858) — At the time: Parliament forced to act — Long term: Bazalgette's sewers transformed London's public health

Key figures

  • 616 — Deaths near the Broad Street pump in 10 days — Snow identified the pattern
  • 83 miles — Length of Bazalgette's sewers built in London — a massive engineering project
