Kaiser Foreign Policy as Cause of WW1 (2025)

AQA 8145 · Paper 1B · Conflict and Tension

16-mark question · How far do you agree · AO1/AO2 · +4 SPaG

The question

‘Kaiser Wilhelm’s foreign policy was the main reason for the start of the First World War.’ How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer. [16 + 4 SPaG marks]

Model answer — Level 4

I strongly agree that Kaiser Wilhelm’s foreign policy was the main reason for the start of the First World War. His policy of Weltpolitik — the ambition to make Germany a world power — systematically dismantled the diplomatic stability of Europe over twenty years, creating the very alliances, arms races, and tensions that made war probable long before any individual crisis arrived. The Alliance System and the arms race, which are often cited as independent causes, were themselves direct consequences of the Kaiser’s aggressive diplomacy.

Kaiser Wilhelm’s Weltpolitik was the most sustained and damaging cause of the First World War. When Wilhelm dismissed Chancellor Bismarck in 1890, he abandoned the cautious Reinsurance Treaty with Russia, immediately driving Russia towards France. The Franco-Russian Alliance of 1894 was a direct consequence of this recklessness. His decision from 1898 to build a large ocean-going battle fleet under Admiral Tirpitz represented a direct challenge to British naval supremacy — the Royal Navy was Britain’s guarantee of national security and imperial supply. This forced Britain into the Entente Cordiale with France in 1904 and the Triple Entente in 1907, completing the encirclement of Germany that Wilhelm had done most to create. His aggressive interventions in the Moroccan Crises of 1905 and 1911 — where Germany had no genuine strategic interest — were deliberate provocations designed to split the Entente and demonstrate German power. Each intervention instead hardened Allied unity and deepened European distrust of Germany.

The Alliance System, often cited as a main cause, was itself a product of Wilhelmine aggression. The Triple Entente of Britain, France, and Russia was not a natural alliance — three such different powers had no obvious reason to align. They did so because the Kaiser’s behaviour made Germany appear an aggressive, destabilising force that any of them might face alone. The Alliance System provided the mechanism by which war spread in 1914, but the system had formed because of the Kaiser’s foreign policy over the preceding two decades. Without Weltpolitik, there would have been no pressing need for the Entente, and the alliance system would not have been so hardened into opposing blocs.

The naval arms race further demonstrates the centrality of the Kaiser’s foreign policy, as it was entirely a product of his personal ambition for German sea power. The 1898 and 1900 Navy Laws initiated a Dreadnought-building race with Britain that consumed enormous resources and created deep mutual hostility. By 1914, Britain had 29 Dreadnoughts to Germany’s 17 — Germany had lost the race it had started and had alienated Britain in the process. Without the Kaiser’s decision to challenge British naval supremacy, there would have been no arms race, no British alignment with France, and no Triple Entente. The Alliance System and the arms race were both expressions of the same underlying problem: that Kaiser Wilhelm’s Weltpolitik had made Germany an unpredictable and aggressive power that Europe’s other states felt compelled to balance against. His foreign policy was not merely one cause among several — it was the cause that generated the others.
