Timing & marks discipline

  • Q1 (4 marks) — quick: two supported points, ~5 min.
  • Q2 (12 marks) — three paragraphs (content · provenance · “Taken together…”), ~15 min.
  • Q3 (8 marks) — a linked account, ~10 min.
  • Q4 (16 marks) — the biggest; plan it first, ~20 min.
    • + SPaG (4 marks) assessed here: capitals, full stops, specialist terms (assassination, mobilisation, alliance).

How to begin each question (memorise this)

3A linked narrative: "this led to… as a result…"

War at sea, America, causes of war

  • War at sea: British naval blockade starved Germany; Battle of Jutland (1916); German U-boats & unrestricted submarine warfare; Lusitania (1915); the convoy system.
  • America's entry (1917): fresh troops, money & supplies; huge morale boost; tipped the balance against an exhausted Germany.
  • Causes of war: the assassination was the trigger, but militarism, alliances, imperialism & nationalism were the deeper causes.

America's impact (Q3) & the assassination as a cause (Q4)

  • America's impact: brought men, money and supplies; its entry was partly caused by U-boat attacks (Lusitania) and the Zimmermann Telegram; helped defeat the 1918 Spring Offensive.
  • Assassination vs deeper causes: Franz Ferdinand's murder (June 1914) was the spark; the alliance system, militarism & Balkan tension were the tinder.

An American cartoon, November 1918, published in a widely read newspaper. The cartoon comments on the end of the First World War.

Source B  (Question 2)

Adapted from an official request sent by the leader of the German navy to the Kaiser in December 1916.

To win the war we must use unrestricted submarine warfare. If we sink 600,000 tons of ships a month it would defeat England which depends on its merchant ships for supplies and food. England is already close to a similar food crisis that we suffer from because of the blockade of our ports. If we start unrestricted submarine attacks immediately we could have peace before the harvest of 1917. In addition, if there were fewer ships, it would be more difficult for troops to get to Europe if America joined the war.

Source C  (Question 2)

A poster produced by the American navy, in January 1917, to recruit sailors. The standing figure represents Germany.

4 marks  |  ~5 minQ1 — Source: How Do You Know?

QUESTIONStudy Source A. Source A supports the view that Germany was a defeated country. How do you know? (Source A is on the Sources Booklet page.)

Technique — pick details + a little knowledge (4 marks)

  • Find two details in Source A showing the view + link each to your knowledge. Be quick.

Model — technique highlighted

I know Source A supports the view that Germany was defeated because it shows signs of collapse — for example exhaustion, shortages or surrender. This supports the view because by 1918 Germany had been weakened by the naval blockade, the failure of the Spring Offensive and the arrival of fresh American troops, forcing it to seek an armistice.

🎓 Examiner says: use details from the source itself, not just general knowledge. Two supported points = full marks.

Your turn: read the real Source A and pick two details. "Source A shows… which supports the view because…"

12 marks  |  ~15 minQ2 — How Useful Are Sources B and C?

QUESTIONHow useful are Sources B and C to an historian studying the war at sea?

Technique — three paragraphs, both sources together

  1. ¶1 — Content (both sources): what does each source show, and what do you know that backs it up? “Source B is useful because it shows… I know this because… Source C is useful because it shows…”
  2. ¶2 — Provenance (both sources): use the nature, origin and purpose — who made each one, when and why — to explain what makes it useful.
  3. ¶3 — “Taken together…”: one short synthesis paragraph — a range of features of the war at sea.

A source is never useless — it always shows something useful, even if it only shows one side or attitude. Always say what each source shows and why that matters.

Model — content & provenance (technique highlighted)

¶1 Content. Source B is useful because it shows Germany’s plan to use unrestricted submarine warfare to sink 600,000 tons of shipping a month and starve Britain. I know this because German U-boats threatened Britain’s supplies, and unrestricted warfare (after the sinking of the Lusitania in 1915) helped bring the USA into the war. Source C is useful because it shows the American navy recruiting sailors against the threat of Germany, which I know because once the USA entered in 1917 its navy helped protect convoys and counter the U-boat campaign.

¶2 Provenance. The fact that Source B is an official request from the leader of the German navy to the Kaiser makes it useful because it shows the strategic thinking behind the submarine campaign. Source C is an American recruitment poster from January 1917, so it is useful for showing how the naval threat was used to mobilise the USA.

¶3 Taken together. Taken together, Sources B and C show a range of features of the war at sea — B shows the German submarine campaign and C the American naval response.

🎓 Examiner says: the strongest answers cover both sources in a content paragraph and a provenance paragraph, then judge with “Taken together…”. Explain what each source shows using its nature, origin and purpose.

Your turn — write ¶3: “Taken together, Sources B and C show a range of features of the war at sea…”

8 marks  |  ~10 minQ3 — Write an Account

QUESTIONWrite an account of how the entry of America had an impact on the First World War.

Technique — an analytical narrative, not a list

  • Show how America's entry changed the war, as a chain of effects.
  • Para 1: the immediate impact (men, money, supplies, morale).
  • Para 2: how this helped bring victory in 1918.

Model paragraph — technique highlighted

America's entry in 1917 had an impact because it gave the Allies a huge boost in men, money and supplies. This mattered because fresh, well-equipped American troops began to arrive just as Britain and France were exhausted, which also lifted Allied morale and worried the Germans.

🎓 Examiner says: a top "account" analyses a sequence — show how America's resources led to the defeat of Germany.

Now continue the chain: "This impact became decisive when the German Spring Offensive (1918) failed and fresh American forces helped the Allies push Germany back in the Hundred Days…"

16 + 4 SPaG  |  ~20 minQ4 — How Far Do You Agree?

QUESTION"The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand was the main reason for the start of the First World War." How far do you agree with this statement? Explain your answer.

Structure (the biggest question — plan first)

  1. Intro: state how far you agree + signpost the factors.
  2. Para 1 — the stated factor: how the assassination triggered the war.
  3. Para 2 & 3 — other factors: the alliance system; militarism & nationalism.
  4. Conclusion — judgement: how far you agree, and why.

Q4 — Model & Writing Frame

Model introduction (signposting) — technique highlighted

I partly agree that the assassination was the main reason, but I will argue that it was only the trigger, and the deeper cause was the alliance system and the tensions built up before 1914. I will explain the stated factor first, then weigh other factors, showing the murder sparked a war that the alliances had made possible.

Model paragraph — the stated factor

The assassination was an important reason because it was the spark that set off the war. When Archduke Franz Ferdinand was murdered in June 1914, Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and declared war. This started the war because it set off the chain of events known as the July Crisis.

Sentence stems for a sustained judgement

  • "Although the assassination triggered the war, it only spread because of the alliance system, which suggests…"
  • "Tensions from militarism and nationalism made war likely anyway, so the murder was a spark not a cause, because…"
  • "Therefore I agree to an extent, but the main reason was… because…"

Common ways students lose marks on Q4

  • Only covering the stated factor.
  • Describing causes with no argument.
  • No clear judgement on "how far".
  • Forgetting SPaG.
