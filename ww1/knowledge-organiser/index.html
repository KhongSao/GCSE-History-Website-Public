The causes of the First World War

Overview and Intro to WW1

Key dates

  • 1871 — Unification of Germany — shifts the balance of power in Europe
  • 28 June 1914 — Assassination of Franz Ferdinand — the trigger for war
  • 4 August 1914 — Britain declares war — the conflict becomes truly world-wide
  • 11 November 1918 — Armistice — end point students need to anchor the whole unit

Key people

  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — German Emperor — his ambition and decisions central to causing the war
  • Gavrilo Princip — Assassin of Franz Ferdinand — representative of Serbian nationalism
  • Franz Ferdinand — Heir to Austria-Hungary — his death sets the July Crisis in motion

Key terms

  • MAIN — Organising framework: Militarism, Alliance System, Imperialism, Nationalism
  • Triple Alliance — Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy — one of the two armed camps
  • Triple Entente — France, Russia, Britain — the opposing bloc
  • Militarism — The culture of building up armed forces — a key underlying cause
  • Nationalism — Pride in nation/ethnic group — destabilised empires, especially in the Balkans

Key events

  • Assassination of Franz Ferdinand (1914) — The immediate trigger — connects underlying causes to the outbreak
  • July Crisis (1914) — The five weeks between assassination and war — shows how alliances escalated
  • Britain declares war (4 Aug 1914) — Germany's invasion of Belgium brings Britain in

Key figures

  • ~17 million — Total dead — establishes the scale and stakes of the war
  • ~20 million — Wounded — human cost beyond fatalities
  • 4 — Major empires collapsed — Ottoman, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, German

Did a New Germany Divide Europe

Key dates

  • 1871 — German unification — a powerful new nation unsettles the established order
  • 1888 — Wilhelm II becomes Kaiser — marks the shift to aggressive foreign policy
  • 1890 — Bismarck dismissed — removal of the cautious hand; Weltpolitik begins
  • 1898 & 1900 — Naval Laws — Germany begins building a fleet to rival Britain

Key people

  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Erratic, ambitious Emperor — his personality shaped Germany's divisive foreign policy
  • Otto von Bismarck — Architect of German unification and cautious diplomacy — contrast with Wilhelm
  • Admiral Tirpitz — Drove the naval expansion programme — key figure in Anglo-German rivalry

Key terms

  • Weltpolitik — World Policy — Wilhelm II's aggressive push for global influence and colonies
  • Realpolitik — Bismarck's pragmatic diplomacy — contrast with Wilhelm's reckless approach
  • Naval Race — Anglo-German competition to build the largest fleet — key source of tension
  • Second Reich — The unified German Empire (1871–1918) — students need this as context

Key events

  • Unification of Germany (1871) — Prussia defeats France — Germany emerges as Europe's strongest land power
  • Dismissal of Bismarck (1890) — Turning point — cautious alliance management replaced by Weltpolitik
  • Naval expansion (1898–1900) — Directly threatens British naval supremacy — drives Britain towards France

Key figures

  • 2x — German industrial output doubled between 1890–1914 — economic basis for its ambitions
  • 800,000 — Size of German army by 1914 — up from ~400,000 in the 1870s
  • 2nd — Germany had the second largest economy in the world by 1914

How Powerful Were the Great Powers

Key dates

  • 1871 — German unification — a new Great Power enters the stage
  • 1905 — Russia defeated by Japan — exposes weakness behind Russian military reputation
  • 1907 — Triple Entente formed — Europe now divided into two armed camps
  • By 1914 — Arms race at its peak — every Great Power mobilising for potential conflict

Key people

  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Drove German military and naval expansion — central to the arms race
  • Tsar Nicholas II — Led Russia — autocratic, weak leadership of a powerful but fragile empire
  • Franz Joseph — Emperor of Austria-Hungary — ageing ruler of a multi-ethnic, unstable empire
  • King Edward VII — Helped broker the Entente Cordiale — personal diplomacy shaped alliances

Key terms

  • Great Powers — Britain, France, Germany, Austria-Hungary, Russia — the key players students must know
  • Arms Race — Competitive military build-up — both naval and land forces
  • Balance of Power — The idea that no one state should dominate Europe — disrupted by Germany's rise
  • Mobilisation — Putting armies on a war footing — once started, very hard to stop

Key events

  • Russo-Japanese War (1905) — Russia's humiliating defeat — reveals the gap between reputation and reality
  • Anglo-German Naval Race (1898–1914) — Britain responds by allying with France — shifts the diplomatic balance
  • Formation of Triple Entente (1907) — Europe locked into two blocs — any crisis risks general war

Key figures

  • 1.4 million — Size of Russian army — largest in Europe, but poorly equipped
  • 2nd largest economy — Germany by 1914 — industrial power underpinning its military ambitions
  • Largest navy — Britain's Royal Navy — the naval race with Germany directly threatened this
  • 2x military spending — European arms spending roughly doubled 1870–1914 — scale of militarism

What Motivated the Alliance System

Key dates

  • 1879 — Dual Alliance (Germany & Austria-Hungary) — the foundation of the Triple Alliance
  • 1882 — Triple Alliance formed — Italy joins Germany and Austria-Hungary
  • 1894 — Franco-Russian Alliance — France ends its isolation; two-front threat for Germany
  • 1904 — Entente Cordiale — Britain and France resolve colonial differences
  • 1907 — Triple Entente — Britain joins France and Russia; Europe divided into two blocs

Key people

  • Bismarck — Architect of the alliance system — intended to isolate France, not cause war
  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Allowed alliances to become aggressive rather than defensive
  • King Edward VII — Personal diplomacy helped shape the Entente Cordiale
  • Tsar Nicholas II — Russia's entry into the Franco-Russian Alliance completed the opposing bloc

Key terms

  • Triple Alliance — Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy — the Central Powers bloc
  • Triple Entente — France, Russia, Britain — the Allied bloc
  • Entente Cordiale — "Friendly agreement" — not a military alliance, but drew Britain closer to France
  • Blank Cheque — Germany's unconditional support for Austria-Hungary in 1914 — escalated the crisis
  • Mutual Defence — The obligation to support allies — why a local crisis became a world war

Key events

  • Dual Alliance (1879) — Bismarck ties Germany to Austria-Hungary — sets the template for the alliance system
  • Franco-Russian Alliance (1894) — Germany now faces potential war on two fronts — drives the Schlieffen Plan
  • Entente Cordiale (1904) — Britain moves away from "splendid isolation" — aligns with France
  • Triple Entente (1907) — Europe locked into two opposing blocs — any spark risks general war

Key figures

  • 3 vs 3 — Triple Alliance vs Triple Entente — Europe split into two roughly matched armed camps
  • 1914 — All major alliances automatically activated within weeks of the assassination

What Was the Most Serious Underlying Source of Tension in Europe

Key dates

  • 1871 — German unification — the root of the power imbalance that caused tension
  • 1905 & 1911 — Moroccan Crises — imperialism and nationalism nearly cause war before 1914
  • 1908 — Bosnian Crisis — Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia; humiliates Russia; inflames nationalism
  • 1912–13 — Balkan Wars — nationalism reshapes the Balkans; Serbia grows stronger and more ambitious

Key people

  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — His pursuit of Weltpolitik drove militarism and imperial rivalry
  • Franz Joseph — Emperor of Austria-Hungary — feared Serbian nationalism as an existential threat
  • Gavrilo Princip — Symbol of South Slav nationalism — the human face of the tension in the Balkans

Key terms

  • MAIN — The four underlying causes — Militarism, Alliance System, Imperialism, Nationalism
  • Militarism — Glorification of military power and preparedness — normalised the idea of war
  • Imperialism — Competition for colonies — especially between Britain, France and Germany
  • Nationalism — Pride in nation or ethnic group — most explosive in the multi-ethnic Balkans
  • Powder Keg — Metaphor for the Balkans — volatile region where underlying tensions were most likely to explode

Key events

  • Moroccan Crises (1905, 1911) — Germany challenges French control of Morocco — imperialism and brinkmanship nearly cause war
  • Bosnian Crisis (1908) — Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia — humiliates Russia; Serbian nationalism intensifies
  • Balkan Wars (1912–13) — Serbia doubles in size — Austria-Hungary increasingly threatened by Serbian nationalism

Key figures

  • 2x — European military spending roughly doubled between 1870–1914 — scale of militarism
  • 800,000 — German army by 1914 — the largest and best-equipped in Europe
  • ~30 million km² — Size of the British Empire — imperialism as a source of rivalry and prestige

Why Was Weltpolitik Worrisome

Key dates

  • 1888 — Wilhelm II becomes Kaiser — start of the aggressive new direction
  • 1890 — Bismarck dismissed — Weltpolitik replaces cautious Realpolitik
  • 1897 — Weltpolitik officially adopted — Germany pursues global power
  • 1898 & 1900 — First and Second Naval Laws — deliberate challenge to British supremacy
  • 1905 & 1911 — Moroccan Crises — Weltpolitik nearly causes war

Key people

  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Driving force behind Weltpolitik — erratic, ambitious, anti-British
  • Bismarck — His dismissal is the turning point — contrast his caution with Wilhelm's recklessness
  • Admiral Tirpitz — Architect of the naval expansion programme

Key terms

  • Weltpolitik — "World Policy" — Wilhelm II's drive for colonies, naval power and global prestige
  • Realpolitik — Bismarck's careful, realistic diplomacy — useful contrast with Weltpolitik
  • Naval Race — Anglo-German competition that pushed Britain towards France
  • Place in the Sun — Phrase used to justify German imperial ambitions

Key events

  • Dismissal of Bismarck (1890) — Removes the brake on German aggression — Weltpolitik can begin
  • First & Second Naval Laws (1898, 1900) — Germany builds a battle fleet — direct threat to British naval dominance
  • Moroccan Crises (1905, 1911) — Weltpolitik in action — Germany challenges France and nearly triggers war

Key figures

  • 800,000 — German army by 1914 — doubled since the 1870s
  • 2nd — Germany's position in world economy by 1914 — industrial power behind Weltpolitik
  • 13 — German dreadnoughts by 1914 vs Britain's 18 — naval race in numbers

Anglo German Rivalry

Key dates

  • 1898 — First Naval Law — Germany begins building a battle fleet
  • 1904 — Entente Cordiale — Britain moves away from isolation towards France
  • 1906 — HMS Dreadnought launched — resets the naval race overnight
  • 1912 — Haldane Mission fails — last attempt to agree naval limits collapses
  • By 1914 — Britain still leads; Germany second — rivalry unresolved at outbreak of war

Key people

  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Personal obsession with naval power — drove the rivalry
  • Admiral Tirpitz — Designed and pushed through the naval expansion programme
  • King Edward VII — Personal diplomacy underpinned the Entente Cordiale
  • Lord Grey — British Foreign Secretary — managed the deteriorating relationship with Germany

Key terms

  • Naval Race — Competitive warship building between Britain and Germany
  • Dreadnought — Revolutionary battleship — made all existing fleets obsolete overnight
  • Two-Power Standard — Britain's policy of maintaining naval dominance over any two rivals
  • Splendid Isolation — Britain's pre-1900 policy of avoiding alliances — ended partly due to German threat
  • Entente Cordiale — 1904 agreement with France — a direct response to German rivalry

Key events

  • First Naval Law (1898) — Germany commits to building a battle fleet — signals intent to challenge Britain
  • HMS Dreadnought launched (1906) — Renders all existing warships obsolete — resets the race
  • Haldane Mission (1912) — Britain attempts to negotiate limits — Germany refuses; rivalry intensifies
  • Entente Cordiale (1904) — Britain aligns with France — rivalry with Germany pushes them together

Key figures

  • 18 vs 13 — British vs German dreadnoughts by 1914 — Britain maintained superiority
  • £50m — British naval spending by 1913 — the escalating cost of the arms race
  • 2:1 — Britain's "Two-Power Standard" — navy must match next two rivals combined

The Moroccan Crises 1905 and 1911

Key dates

  • 1905 — First Moroccan Crisis — Wilhelm II lands at Tangier; challenges French control
  • 1906 — Algeciras Conference — Germany humiliated; France and Britain draw closer
  • 1911 — Agadir Crisis — Germany sends gunboat Panther; second challenge to France
  • 1911 — Lloyd George's Mansion House speech — Britain signals it will back France

Key people

  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Personally landed at Tangier in 1905 to signal German interest
  • Lloyd George — Mansion House speech (1911) — warned Germany Britain would support France
  • Théophile Delcassé — French Foreign Minister whose removal Germany demanded in 1905

Key terms

  • Brinkmanship — Pushing a confrontation to the edge of war to force concessions
  • Gunboat Diplomacy — Using naval threat to achieve political goals — the Agadir Crisis
  • Algeciras Conference — 1906 international meeting — resolved First Crisis; humiliated Germany
  • Sphere of Influence — An area where one power has dominant control — Morocco was France's
  • Agadir Crisis — Second Moroccan Crisis 1911 — Germany's last major pre-war confrontation

Key events

  • First Moroccan Crisis (1905) — Wilhelm II challenges French control of Morocco — nearly causes war
  • Algeciras Conference (1906) — Germany isolated diplomatically — backfire strengthens Entente
  • Agadir Crisis (1911) — Germany sends Panther — Britain backs France; Germany backs down again

Key figures

  • 13 nations — Attended Algeciras Conference — Germany isolated; only Austria-Hungary backed her
  • 1 — Gunboat (Panther) sent to Agadir — the provocative act that triggered the crisis

The Bosnian Crisis

Key dates

  • 1908 — Austria-Hungary annexes Bosnia — the key event of the crisis
  • 1908 — Russia humiliated — backs down; stores up resentment for 1914
  • 1908 — Serbia furious — annexation blocks Serbian expansion to the Adriatic

Key people

  • Franz Joseph — Emperor of Austria-Hungary — annexation was his government's decision
  • Aehrenthal — Austrian Foreign Minister — drove the annexation; deceived Russia
  • Izvolsky — Russian Foreign Minister — felt double-crossed; humiliated
  • Tsar Nicholas II — Backed down in 1908 — determined to act in 1914 when Serbia was threatened again

Key terms

  • Annexation — Formally incorporating a territory into another state — what Austria-Hungary did to Bosnia
  • Pan-Slavism — The idea of Slavic unity — Russia saw itself as protector of Slavic peoples
  • Balkans — Region of south-east Europe — repeatedly called the "powder keg" of Europe
  • Humiliation — Key word — Russia's forced climb-down stored up bitterness used in 1914

Key events

  • Annexation of Bosnia (1908) — Austria-Hungary formalises control — inflames Serbian nationalism; humiliates Russia
  • Russia backs down (1908) — Not ready for war — but determined not to be humiliated again (crucial for 1914)
  • Serbia's reaction — Serbia vows revenge — pan-Slavic tension escalates toward 1914

Key figures

  • ~2 million — Slavic population of Bosnia — why Serbia and Russia objected so strongly
  • 0 — Compensation given to Russia — Izvolsky felt betrayed by Aehrenthal

Balkan Wars

Key dates

  • 1912 — First Balkan War — Balkan League defeats the Ottoman Empire
  • 1913 — Second Balkan War — former allies fight over the spoils
  • 1913 — Treaty of Bucharest — Serbia emerges as the big winner

Key people

  • Serbia's leaders — Serbian ambition and growth directly threatened Austria-Hungary
  • Franz Joseph — Austria-Hungary saw Serbian expansion as an existential threat
  • Tsar Nicholas II — Russia backed Serbia — pan-Slavic solidarity increased tension with Austria

Key terms

  • Balkan League — Alliance of Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece, Montenegro — formed to fight the Ottomans
  • Ottoman Empire — The "sick man of Europe" — its collapse destabilised the whole region
  • Pan-Slavism — Slavic nationalism energised by Serbian victories — alarmed Austria-Hungary
  • Nationalism — The wars showed how nationalism could redraw the map — dangerous precedent

Key events

  • First Balkan War (1912) — Balkan League defeats Ottoman Empire — redrew the map of south-east Europe
  • Second Balkan War (1913) — Former allies fight over territory — Bulgaria loses; Serbia gains further
  • Treaty of Bucharest (1913) — Serbia emerges greatly strengthened — Austria-Hungary alarmed

Key figures

  • 2x — Serbia roughly doubled in size as a result of the Balkan Wars
  • ~500,000 — Ottoman troops defeated — empire loses most of its European territory

Key dates

  • 28 June 1914 — Assassination of Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo — the spark that triggers war
  • 23 July 1914 — Austria-Hungary issues ultimatum to Serbia
  • 28 July 1914 — Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia

Key people

  • Gavrilo Princip — 19-year-old Bosnian Serb — pulled the trigger; symbol of South Slav nationalism
  • Franz Ferdinand — Heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne — his death the trigger for WWI
  • Colonel Apis — Head of the Black Hand — organised the assassination plot
  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Issued the Blank Cheque — gave Austria-Hungary unconditional German support

Key terms

  • Black Hand — Serbian nationalist secret society — organised the assassination
  • Ultimatum — Austria-Hungary's demands to Serbia — designed to be rejected
  • Blank Cheque — Germany's unconditional backing of Austria-Hungary — escalated the crisis
  • Nationalism — The assassination was an act of South Slav nationalism — connect this to MAIN

Key events

  • Assassination (28 June 1914) — Franz Ferdinand and Sophie shot by Princip in Sarajevo
  • Austrian ultimatum (23 July) — Extreme demands Serbia could not fully accept — designed to justify war
  • Austria declares war on Serbia (28 July) — July Crisis becomes a war — alliances now begin to activate

Key figures

  • 7 — Assassins positioned along the Appel Quay — coordinated Black Hand plot
  • 2 — Attempts on the day — first failed; Princip succeeded on a second encounter

July Crisis (1914)

Key dates

  • 28 June 1914 — Assassination — the starting gun for the crisis
  • 23 July 1914 — Austrian ultimatum issued to Serbia
  • 28 July 1914 — Austria declares war on Serbia
  • 1 August 1914 — Germany declares war on Russia
  • 3 August 1914 — Germany declares war on France
  • 4 August 1914 — Britain declares war on Germany — after invasion of Belgium

Key people

  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Issued Blank Cheque — fateful decision that removed Austrian restraint
  • Franz Joseph — Emperor of Austria-Hungary — determined to crush Serbia
  • Tsar Nicholas II — Ordered Russian mobilisation — could not back down again after 1908
  • Sir Edward Grey — British Foreign Secretary — tried and failed to broker peace
  • Bethmann Hollweg — German Chancellor — accepted the risk of wider war

Key terms

  • July Crisis — The five-week period from assassination to general war
  • Blank Cheque — Germany's unconditional backing of Austria — removed the last brake on escalation
  • Mobilisation — Putting armies on a war footing — once begun, almost impossible to stop
  • Ultimatum — Austria's demands to Serbia — deliberately impossible to fully accept
  • War by timetable — Idea that military plans drove escalation faster than diplomats could act

Key events

  • Austrian ultimatum (23 July) — Designed to be rejected — gives Austria pretext for war
  • Austria declares war on Serbia (28 July) — Activates the alliance system
  • German mobilisation (1 Aug) — Schlieffen Plan kicks in — must attack France first via Belgium
  • Britain declares war (4 Aug) — Germany's invasion of Belgium triggers British entry

Key figures

  • 6 weeks — From assassination to Britain entering the war — how fast alliances escalated a local dispute
  • 5 — Major powers at war within weeks of a single assassination

The First World War: stalemate

The Schlieffen Plan

Key dates

  • 1905 — Schlieffen Plan devised — Germany's solution to the two-front war problem
  • 1914 — Modified by Moltke and put into action
  • August 1914 — Invasion of Belgium — brings Britain into the war
  • September 1914 — Battle of the Marne — plan fails; trench warfare begins

Key people

  • Alfred von Schlieffen — Devised the plan — Chief of German General Staff 1891–1906
  • Helmuth von Moltke (the Younger) — Modified and implemented the plan — blamed for its failure
  • Kaiser Wilhelm II — Ultimately responsible for authorising the plan's execution

Key terms

  • Schlieffen Plan — Germany's strategy to avoid a two-front war — knock out France fast, then deal with Russia
  • Two-Front War — Germany's nightmare — fighting France and Russia simultaneously
  • Belgian neutrality — Britain's guarantee of Belgium — violated by Germany; brought Britain into the war
  • War by timetable — Military plans left no room for diplomacy once mobilisation began

Key events

  • Plan devised (1905) — Schlieffen's response to the Franco-Russian Alliance
  • Modified by Moltke (1914) — Weakened the right flank — contributed to its failure
  • Invasion of Belgium (Aug 1914) — Triggers British entry into the war
  • Battle of the Marne (Sept 1914) — Germany stopped — plan fails; Western Front stalemates into trenches

Key figures

  • 6 weeks — Time allowed to defeat France before turning east to fight Russia
  • 90% — Proportion of German forces to go west first under the plan
  • 700km — Length of trenches that result from the plan's failure

Trench Warfare - Weapons

Key dates

  • Late 1914 — Trenches established — race to the sea ends; stalemate begins
  • April 1915 — First large-scale use of poison gas — Second Battle of Ypres
  • September 1916 — Tanks first used in battle — at the Somme

Key people

  • Haig — Advocated use of new technology — tanks and creeping barrages
  • German high command — Developed mustard gas and defensive trench systems

Key terms

  • No Man's Land — The ground between opposing trenches — deadly and almost uncrossable
  • Artillery — Heavy guns — responsible for the majority of casualties in WW1
  • Poison Gas — Chlorine, then phosgene, then mustard gas — psychological as well as physical weapon
  • Machine Gun — Made frontal attacks catastrophically costly — key reason for stalemate
  • Tank — New weapon introduced 1916 — eventually helped break the deadlock
  • Barbed Wire — Slowed attackers in No Man's Land — made them vulnerable to machine guns

Key events

  • Race to the Sea (1914) — Both sides try to outflank each other — ends in trench stalemate
  • First use of gas (April 1915) — Germany uses chlorine at Second Ypres — changes the nature of warfare
  • Tanks at the Somme (Sept 1916) — First use in battle — limited success but signals future potential

Key figures

  • 700km — Length of the Western Front trench system
  • ~1.3 million — Casualties caused by gas attacks across the war
  • 49 — Tanks used on first day at the Somme — mostly broke down

Trench Warfare - Trenches

Key dates

  • Late 1914 — Trench system established along the Western Front
  • 1914–1918 — Four years of stalemate — trenches defined the war

Key people

  • Haig — Commander who ordered offensives from behind the lines — criticism focuses here
  • The ordinary soldier (Tommy) — Central to this lesson — students should understand life at ground level

Key terms

  • Trench system — Network of front, support and reserve trenches — the architecture of stalemate
  • No Man's Land — Ground between opposing trenches — deadly, shell-cratered, wire-strewn
  • Stalemate — Neither side able to advance — the defining condition of the Western Front
  • Stand-to — Dawn and dusk alert — soldiers ready for attack at most vulnerable times
  • Shell shock — Psychological trauma from sustained bombardment — not understood or treated well
  • Dugout — Underground shelter in trench walls — protection from artillery

Key events

  • Race to the Sea (1914) — Both sides dig in after the Schlieffen Plan fails — establishes the trench system
  • Daily trench routine — Stand-to, repairs, patrols, raids — the grinding reality students must understand

Key figures

  • 700km — Total length of Allied trench system on the Western Front
  • ~50m — Minimum distance between some British and German front lines
  • 3 — Lines of trenches — front, support, reserve — the full system

War of Attrition - Verdun (1916)

Key dates

  • February 1916 — Battle of Verdun begins — Germany's deliberate war of attrition
  • July 1916 — Somme offensive launched partly to relieve pressure on Verdun
  • December 1916 — Battle of Verdun ends — France holds; Germany's strategy failed

Key people

  • Falkenhayn — German Chief of Staff — devised the deliberate attrition strategy at Verdun
  • Pétain — French commander at Verdun — "They shall not pass"; national hero
  • Joffre — French C-in-C — pressed Britain to launch the Somme to relieve Verdun

Key terms

  • Attrition — Strategy of wearing down the enemy's resources and will — Falkenhayn's explicit aim
  • Bleed France white — Falkenhayn's stated goal — Verdun chosen as a place France could not afford to lose
  • War of attrition — The wider strategic concept — grind the enemy down rather than break through

Key events

  • Battle of Verdun begins (Feb 1916) — Germany attacks — intends to drain French manpower rather than capture territory
  • Battle of the Somme (July 1916) — Britain attacks partly to relieve French at Verdun — the battles are linked
  • Verdun ends (Dec 1916) — Germany fails to "bleed France white" — ~equal losses on both sides

Key figures

  • ~700,000 — Total French and German casualties at Verdun
  • ~300,000 — Killed — one of the bloodiest battles in history
  • 10 months — Duration of the battle — the longest of WW1

War of Attrition - Somme (1916)

Key dates

  • 1 July 1916 — First day of the Somme — the worst day in British military history
  • July–November 1916 — Full duration of the battle
  • September 1916 — Tanks used for the first time — at Flers-Courcelette

Key people

  • Douglas Haig — Commanded the Somme — most contested figure; students must evaluate his decisions
  • Rawlinson — Commander of the 4th Army — planned the first-day tactics
  • Lloyd George — British PM and fierce critic of Haig — key for interpretation questions

Key terms

  • Attrition — Wearing down German reserves — Haig's justification for the offensive
  • Creeping barrage — Artillery fire advancing just ahead of infantry — introduced mid-battle
  • Pals Battalions — Units of men from the same town — meant whole communities lost men on one day
  • Shell shock — Psychological trauma — the Somme generated huge numbers of cases
  • Over the top — The moment soldiers left the trench to advance across No Man's Land

Key events

  • Week-long bombardment (June 1916) — Intended to destroy German defences — largely failed
  • First day (1 July 1916) — Catastrophic casualties — Germans sheltered in deep dugouts
  • Tanks introduced (Sept 1916) — First use of tanks in warfare — limited but significant
  • Battle ends (Nov 1916) — Haig claims attritional victory — Lloyd George deeply sceptical

Key figures

  • 57,470 — British casualties on the first day — ~19,000 killed
  • ~420,000 — Total British casualties across the battle
  • ~500,000 — German casualties — Haig's attrition argument
  • ~200,000 — French casualties
  • 10km — Maximum ground gained — at enormous human cost

War of Attrition - Passchendaele (1917)

Key dates

  • July 1917 — Third Battle of Ypres (Passchendaele) begins
  • August 1917 — Unusually heavy rain — turns battlefield into a muddy swamp
  • November 1917 — Village of Passchendaele finally captured — battle ends

Key people

  • Douglas Haig — Ordered and continued the offensive — the sharpest point of the "Butcher Haig" debate
  • Gough then Plumer — Successive British commanders — Plumer's more methodical approach came too late
  • Lloyd George — Bitterly opposed the offensive — tried and failed to stop it

Key terms

  • Passchendaele — Village that gave its name to the battle — symbol of futile sacrifice
  • Third Ypres — Official name — the third major battle around Ypres
  • Attrition — Haig's continued justification — wearing down German reserves
  • Mud — Central to the battle's legacy — soldiers drowned in shell craters; movement near impossible

Key events

  • Battle begins (July 1917) — Haig launches offensive in Flanders despite objections from Lloyd George
