Elizabeth's court & Parliament

Intro and Overview

Key dates

  • 1558 — Elizabeth I becomes queen — inherits a divided nation (religion, finance, war)
  • 1559 — Elizabethan Religious Settlement — attempted to end religious division
  • 1569 — Rebellion of the Northern Earls — first major Catholic threat
  • 1587 — Mary Queen of Scots executed — removed the main Catholic claimant
  • 1588 — Spanish Armada — the greatest external threat of the reign
  • 1601 — Essex's Rebellion — sign of weakness at the end of the reign
  • 1603 — Elizabeth I dies — end of the Tudor dynasty

Key people

  • Elizabeth I — 1558–1603 — navigated religion, marriage, plots and war; the "Virgin Queen"
  • William Cecil (Lord Burghley) — Chief adviser — stable, loyal, pragmatic; served for 40 years
  • Francis Walsingham — Spymaster — uncovered the Babington Plot; protected Elizabeth from Catholic threats

Key terms

  • Protestant — Elizabeth's religion — the Church of England, established by the Settlement
  • Catholic — The rival religion — threat from Spain, Pope, and internal plots
  • Privy Council — Elizabeth's key governing body — ~19 advisers
  • Patronage — System of rewards — Elizabeth controlled her nobility through giving/withholding favour

Key events

  • Religious Settlement (1559) — At the time: attempted to resolve religious division — Long term: remained under challenge from Catholics and Puritans
  • Spanish Armada (1588) — At the time: greatest threat to England — Long term: its defeat became defining moment of the reign

Key figures

  • 45 years — Length of Elizabeth's reign — one of the longest and most stable in English history
  • ~3 million — Population of England in 1558
  • 13 — Number of times Elizabeth called Parliament in 45 years — she kept it controlled

How Did Government Work in Elizabeth’s Reign

Key dates

  • 1558 — Elizabeth's accession — government structure established from the start
  • 1571 onwards — Increasing parliamentary assertiveness — tension in government
  • 1601 — Monopolies crisis in Parliament — shows the limits of royal prerogative

Key people

  • William Cecil (Lord Burghley) — Chief Secretary and Lord Treasurer — the backbone of Elizabethan government
  • Robert Dudley (Earl of Leicester) — Favourite — political influence at court
  • Robert Cecil — Burghley's son — took over as chief minister in the 1590s

Key terms

  • Privy Council — Elizabeth's key governing body — made day-to-day decisions
  • Parliament — Lords and Commons — needed for taxation; could not make law without it
  • Great Chain of Being — Belief that God set a hierarchy — justified Elizabeth's authority
  • Justices of the Peace (JPs) — Local governors — enforced law in counties without a standing bureaucracy
  • Royal prerogative — Elizabeth's personal powers — she decided foreign policy, religion, marriage alone

Key events

  • Government structure (from 1558) — At the time: a monarchy with limited parliamentary power — Long term: growing tension between Crown and Parliament
  • Monopolies crisis (1601) — At the time: Parliament challenged royal grants of monopolies — Long term: foreshadows later Stuart/Parliament conflict

Key figures

  • ~19 — Members of the Privy Council — small, inner circle of government
  • 13 — Times Elizabeth called Parliament in 45 years — she avoided it where possible

Court Life and Influential People

Key dates

  • 1558 onwards — Elizabeth's court established — patronage system central to power
  • 1590s — Court factionalism — Essex vs Cecil — signs of political instability

Key people

  • Robert Dudley (Earl of Leicester) — Elizabeth's great favourite; possibly a romantic relationship; political ally for 30 years
  • Robert Devereux (Earl of Essex) — Late favourite — charismatic but reckless; led to rebellion 1601
  • William Cecil (Lord Burghley) — Most powerful adviser — stable counterpoint to court favourites

Key terms

  • Court — The royal household — the centre of politics, culture, and power
  • Patronage — Elizabeth's tool for rewarding loyalty — access to titles, land, monopolies, positions
  • Privy Chamber — The innermost part of the court — only the most trusted courtiers allowed
  • Royal progresses — Annual summer tours of the country — saved money; reinforced loyalty; projected power
  • Favourites — Courtiers like Dudley and Essex — could gain or lose enormous influence

Key events

  • Elizabeth's annual progresses — At the time: toured the country staying at nobles' houses — Long term: saved money and reinforced the loyalty of the nobility
  • Essex's rise and fall (1590s) — At the time: factional rivalry at court — Long term: led to Essex's Rebellion 1601; shows dangers of patronage

Key figures

  • ~1,500 — Courtiers at Elizabeth's court — enormous cost; maintained by patronage

Was Elizabeth’s Relationship with Parliament Co-operation or Conflict

Key dates

  • 1566 — Parliament presses Elizabeth to marry and settle the succession
  • 1571 — Peter Wentworth imprisoned for claiming free speech in Parliament
  • 1601 — Monopolies debate — MPs challenge royal grants; Elizabeth gives "Golden Speech"

Key people

  • Peter Wentworth — MP — argued for free speech in Parliament; imprisoned twice by Elizabeth
  • Robert Cecil — Late Elizabethan chief minister — managed Parliament for the queen

Key terms

  • Parliament — Lords and Commons — Elizabeth needed it for taxation and some legislation
  • Royal prerogative — Powers reserved for the monarch — marriage, foreign policy, religion
  • Monopolies — Exclusive rights to sell goods — Parliament challenged these in 1601
  • Free speech — Peter Wentworth argued MPs should have free speech — Elizabeth disagreed
  • Golden Speech — Elizabeth's 1601 speech to Parliament — defused monopolies crisis through charm

Key events

  • Parliament presses succession (1566) — At the time: MPs demanded Elizabeth marry — Long term: shows Parliament dared challenge the monarch on key issues
  • Peter Wentworth imprisoned (1571) — At the time: Elizabeth asserted her prerogative — Long term: shows limits of parliamentary freedom
  • Monopolies crisis (1601) — At the time: Parliament challenged royal grants — Long term: Elizabeth defused it but the issue remained; Stuart kings would fail to do the same

Key figures

  • 13 — Times Elizabeth called Parliament in 45 years — she minimised its role

Why Was It Important for Elizabeth to Marry

Key dates

  • 1559–81 — Various marriage negotiations — Elizabeth never committed
  • 1566 — Parliament demands she name a successor — pressure at its height
  • 1581 — Final serious suitor (Duke of Anjou) rejected — she remained the "Virgin Queen"

Key people

  • Robert Dudley (Earl of Leicester) — Closest Elizabeth came to a personal choice — but she never married him
  • Duke of Anjou — Last serious suitor — French Catholic; rejected partly on religious grounds
  • Philip II of Spain — Early suitor (also her half-sister Mary's widower) — rejected; became her greatest enemy

Key terms

  • Succession — Who would inherit the throne — the central political issue Elizabeth refused to resolve
  • Dynastic marriage — Marriage as a political/diplomatic tool — the expectation for any monarch
  • Virgin Queen — Elizabeth's image after choosing not to marry — turned weakness into strength
  • Protestant succession — Fear that a Catholic might inherit — what Parliament wanted resolved

Key events

  • Marriage negotiations (1559–81) — At the time: constant diplomatic activity — Long term: Elizabeth used marriage negotiations as a tool without ever committing
  • Duke of Anjou rejected (1581) — At the time: end of marriage negotiations — Long term: succession remained unresolved; James VI of Scotland inherited in 1603

Key figures

  • ~17 — Number of foreign suitors Elizabeth received proposals from
  • 45 years — Reign length — Elizabeth outlived the succession crisis she never resolved

The Strength of Elizabeth’s Authority at the End of Her Reign

Key dates

  • 1594–97 — Four successive harvest failures — widespread poverty and discontent
  • 1599 — Essex fails in Ireland — Elizabeth's favourite humiliated
  • 1601 — Essex's Rebellion — February; suppressed; Essex executed
  • 1601 — Monopolies crisis in Parliament — Elizabeth's "Golden Speech" defuses it
  • 1603 — Elizabeth dies — end of Tudor dynasty

Key people

  • Robert Devereux (Essex) — Reckless favourite — rebellion showed the dangers of patronage
  • Robert Cecil — Loyal chief minister at end of reign — quietly managed the succession to James VI
  • Elizabeth I — Aged but still authoritative — her "Golden Speech" shows political skill

Key terms

  • Monopolies — Exclusive trading rights — increasingly resented by Parliament
  • Essex's Rebellion — February 1601 — attempt by a favourite to seize power
  • Golden Speech — Elizabeth's 1601 speech to Parliament — charmed MPs on monopolies; last major speech
  • Succession — Still unresolved at her death — James VI of Scotland inherited

Key events

  • Essex's Rebellion (1601) — At the time: quickly suppressed — Long term: shows Elizabeth's authority remained; but also that late-reign favouritism caused problems
  • Monopolies crisis (1601) — At the time: Parliament angry about monopolies — Long term: Elizabeth conceded gracefully; "Golden Speech"

Key figures

  • ~300 — Followers in Essex's rebellion — rapidly collapsed
  • 45 years — The reign that appeared to end in weakness — but Elizabeth managed to stay in control

What Does Essex’s Rebellion Tell Us About Government and Politics

Key dates

  • 1596 — Essex — victory at Cadiz; height of his popularity
  • 1599 — Essex fails in Ireland — unauthorised peace with Tyrone; storms back to court
  • February 1601 — Essex's Rebellion — attempted to seize power; quickly collapsed
  • 25 February 1601 — Essex executed — Elizabeth signed the warrant

Key people

  • Robert Devereux (2nd Earl of Essex) — Favourite turned rebel — charismatic, reckless; executed aged 35
  • Elizabeth I — Signed the execution warrant — shows authority was maintained even at cost of personal grief
  • Robert Cecil — Essex's rival — used Essex's failure to consolidate his own power

Key terms

  • Essex's Rebellion — 1601 — a favourite's attempt to seize control; showed limitations of patronage politics
  • Treason — Essex was tried and convicted of treason — Elizabeth signed his death warrant
  • Patronage — System Essex had benefited from — his rebellion was partly caused by Elizabeth withdrawing favour
  • Factionalism — Court divided between Essex and Cecil factions — caused political instability

Key events

  • Essex in Ireland (1599) — At the time: humiliating failure — Long term: destroyed his political credit; set the path to rebellion
  • Essex's Rebellion (February 1601) — At the time: quickly failed — Long term: shows royal authority remained intact; but also the dangers of patronage politics

Key figures

  • ~300 — Followers in Essex's march through London — almost no public support

Review and Assessment

  • 1558 — Elizabeth's accession — government, religion, marriage all become immediate challenges
  • 1559 — Religious Settlement — attempted to end religious conflict
  • 1569 — Rebellion of Northern Earls — first major Catholic challenge
  • 1588 — Spanish Armada — greatest external threat
  • 1601 — Essex's Rebellion — late-reign challenge; Elizabeth survives

Key people

  • Elizabeth I — Navigated religion, marriage, plots, and war — her reign defined as England's "Golden Age"
  • William Cecil (Lord Burghley) — Stable chief minister for 40 years — backbone of Elizabethan government
  • Francis Walsingham — Spymaster — uncovered Catholic plots; protected Elizabeth

Key terms

  • Privy Council — Key governing body — approx 19 members
  • Parliament — Lords and Commons — needed for taxation; sometimes conflicted with Crown
  • Patronage — System of royal rewards — controlled loyalty
  • Religious Settlement — 1559 — attempted middle way between Catholic and Protestant extremes

Key events

  • Religious Settlement (1559) — At the time: established the Church of England — Long term: challenged by Catholics and Puritans
  • Spanish Armada (1588) — At the time: greatest threat — Long term: defeat became national myth of Elizabethan achievement

Key figures

  • 45 years — Length of Elizabeth's reign — remarkable stability

Religion & threats at home

Elizabethan Settlement

Key dates

  • 1558 — Elizabeth inherits a country religiously divided — Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I had swung the pendulum
  • 1559 — Act of Supremacy — Elizabeth becomes Supreme Governor of the Church of England
  • 1559 — Act of Uniformity — one Prayer Book required in all churches

Key people

  • Elizabeth I — Designed a deliberately vague Settlement — "a window into no man's soul"
  • Archbishop Matthew Parker — First Archbishop of Canterbury under the Settlement — moderate, loyal
  • Pope Pius IV — Issued a papal bull in 1570 excommunicating Elizabeth — directly challenged the Settlement

Key terms

  • Act of Supremacy — 1559 — Elizabeth as Supreme Governor (not Head) of the Church
  • Act of Uniformity — 1559 — one form of service required in all churches
  • Supreme Governor — Elizabeth's title — she was careful not to call herself "Head" (too strong for some Protestants)
  • Recusant — A Catholic who refused to attend Church of England services — fined under the Act
  • Prayer Book — The authorised form of service — a compromise between Catholic and Protestant forms

Key events

  • Act of Supremacy (1559) — At the time: made Elizabeth head of the Church — Long term: challenged by Catholics (Pope refused to accept) and Puritans (not Protestant enough)
  • Act of Uniformity (1559) — At the time: enforced one form of worship — Long term: moderately successful but never fully accepted by extremes

Key figures

  • ~400 — Clergy who refused the Oath of Supremacy and lost their positions
  • 12 pence — Fine for not attending Church of England services (recusancy fine) — low at first, raised later

Revolt - Rebellion of Northern Earls

Key dates

  • 1568 — Mary Queen of Scots flees to England — Elizabeth imprisons her; she becomes a Catholic focus
  • November 1569 — Rebellion of the Northern Earls begins
  • December 1569 — Rebellion collapses — without expected Spanish support it fell apart

Key people

  • Earl of Westmorland — Co-leader of the rebellion — fled to the Continent after its failure
  • Earl of Northumberland — Co-leader — executed 1572
  • Mary Queen of Scots — The intended replacement for Elizabeth — never freed; executed 1587

Key terms

  • Rebellion of the Northern Earls — 1569 — Catholic earls tried to replace Elizabeth with Mary Queen of Scots
  • Mary Queen of Scots — Cousin of Elizabeth — Catholic claimant to the throne; trigger for rebellion
  • Recusant — Catholic who refused to attend Church of England — many in the North
  • Northern lords — More conservative, Catholic sympathies — different from the Protestant South

Key events

  • Mary QoS arrives in England (1568) — At the time: Elizabeth imprisons her — Long term: created a constant Catholic threat for 19 years
  • Rebellion of the Northern Earls (1569) — At the time: quickly collapsed — Long term: showed the danger of Mary's presence; led to harsher laws against Catholics

Key figures

  • ~6,000 — Rebel troops raised by the Northern Earls
  • ~700 — Ordinary rebels executed as punishment — to deter future rebellions

Why Was Catholicism a Threat

Key dates

  • 1570 — Papal Bull (Regnans in Excelsis) — Pope excommunicates Elizabeth; freed Catholics from loyalty to her
  • 1572 — Ridolfi Plot — plan to assassinate Elizabeth and replace with Mary QoS
  • 1583 — Throckmorton Plot — similar Catholic assassination plot
  • 1586 — Babington Plot — foiled by Walsingham; directly led to Mary's execution
  • 1587 — Mary Queen of Scots executed — removed the main Catholic claimant

Key people

  • Francis Walsingham — Spymaster — uncovered Throckmorton and Babington Plots; used double agents
  • Mary Queen of Scots — Catholic claimant — imprisoned for 19 years; executed 1587
  • Anthony Babington — Led the 1586 plot — executed; his letters proved Mary's complicity

Key terms

  • Papal Bull (Regnans in Excelsis) — 1570 — Pope excommunicated Elizabeth; made English Catholics potential traitors
  • Ridolfi/Throckmorton/Babington Plots — Three Catholic plots to kill Elizabeth — all foiled
  • Mary Queen of Scots — The Catholic alternative to Elizabeth — executed 1587 after Babington Plot
  • Seminary priests — Priests trained abroad (Douai) sent to England secretly — kept Catholicism alive

Key events

  • Papal Bull (1570) — At the time: Catholics now had a religious duty to rebel — Long term: made all Catholics potential traitors in English eyes
  • Babington Plot (1586) — At the time: final proof of Mary's involvement in plots — Long term: led directly to her execution
  • Mary QoS executed (1587) — At the time: removed the Catholic claimant — Long term: enraged Philip II; directly contributed to the Armada

Key figures

  • ~200 — Catholic priests trained abroad who secretly entered England
  • 183 — Catholics executed for treason under Elizabeth (including priests)

Why Were the Puritans a Threat to Elizabeth’s Government

Key dates

  • 1576 — Archbishop Grindal refuses to suppress Prophesyings — Elizabeth suspends him
  • 1579 — John Stubbs has his right hand cut off for publishing against Elizabeth's French marriage
  • 1583 — Archbishop Whitgift appointed — harshly suppresses Puritan practices
  • 1593 — Act against Seditious Words — Separatists (most extreme Puritans) face execution

Key people

  • Archbishop Grindal — 1576 — refused to suppress Prophesyings; suspended by Elizabeth
  • Archbishop Whitgift — 1583 — Elizabeth's chosen enforcer against Puritanism
  • John Stubbs — 1579 — had hand cut off for anti-French marriage pamphlet
  • Henry Greenwood — Leading Separatist — executed 1593

Key terms

  • Puritan — Extreme Protestant — wanted to "purify" the Church of Catholic remnants
  • Prophesying — Unauthorised Puritan meetings for Bible study and preaching — Elizabeth ordered them banned
  • Presbyterian — Form of church government by elected elders — challenged the Queen's authority over the Church
  • Separatist — The most extreme Puritans — wanted to leave the Church of England entirely
  • Sedition — Challenging the monarch's authority — what Elizabeth accused extreme Puritans of

Key events

  • Grindal suspended (1576) — At the time: Archbishop refused to obey the queen on Prophesyings — Long term: showed Puritanism challenged royal authority, not just religious practice
  • Stubbs's hand cut off (1579) — At the time: extreme punishment for publishing criticism — Long term: shows how seriously Elizabeth took challenges to her authority
  • Whitgift's crackdown (1583) — At the time: systematic suppression of Puritan clergy — Long term: drove some Puritans underground; Separatists towards Pilgrim Fathers

Life in Elizabethan times

Living Standards and Fashions

Key dates

  • 1560s–1590s — Elizabethan "Golden Age" — economic growth and cultural flourishing for the wealthy
  • 1577 — William Harrison's Description of England — detailed account of Elizabethan living standards

Key people

  • William Harrison — 1534–93 — clergyman; wrote Description of England 1577; key source on Elizabethan living
  • Elizabeth I — Her court set fashion trends — elaborate dress as an expression of power

Key terms

  • Gentry — The landowning class — below nobility but above yeomen; increasingly important
  • Yeomen — Independent farmers — growing in prosperity; middling sort
  • Sumptuary laws — Laws controlling what people could wear based on their status
  • Prodigy houses — Grand country houses built by wealthy Elizabethan gentry to impress the queen
  • Great Chain of Being — God-given hierarchy — everyone had their place; social mobility was dangerous

Key events

  • Building of prodigy houses — At the time: wealthy gentry showing status — Long term: architectural legacy of Elizabethan prosperity
  • Enclosures (growing problem) — At the time: gentry benefited; poor were dispossessed — Long term: caused the poverty crisis of the 1590s

Key figures

  • ~2% — Proportion of the population who were gentry — but held most of the wealth
  • ~3 million — Total population of England — compared to ~90 million in France

What Were Theatres Like in Elizabethan Times

Key dates

  • 1576 — The Theatre — first purpose-built playhouse in England (Shoreditch)
  • 1599 — Globe Theatre built — Bankside, Southwark
  • 1613 — Globe burns down — rebuilt 1614
  • 1997 — Shakespeare's Globe opened — modern reconstruction

Key people

  • James Burbage — Built The Theatre 1576 — the first purpose-built playhouse
  • Richard Burbage — James's son — the leading actor at the Globe; played Shakespeare's great roles
  • William Shakespeare — Globe playwright — his works are the defining legacy of Elizabethan theatre

Key terms

  • Globe Theatre — Open-air theatre, Bankside — associated with Shakespeare; key site of Elizabethan culture
  • Groundlings — Standing audience in the yard — the poorest and most rowdy section
  • Galleries — Covered seating areas — three tiers; more expensive
  • Tiring house — Backstage area — actors changed costume; used for entrances/exits
  • Thrust stage — Stage that jutted into the audience — allowed close relationship between actors and viewers

Key events

  • Globe built (1599) — At the time: new theatrical space in Southwark — Long term: became the home of Shakespeare's greatest plays
  • Globe burns (1613) — At the time: a cannon set the thatched roof alight during a performance of Henry VIII

Key figures

  • ~3,000 — Capacity of the Globe — one of the largest entertainment venues in Europe
  • 1 penny — Cost for a groundling (standing) — accessible to all classes
  • 6 pennies — Cost for a seat in the galleries

Attitudes to the Theatre - Plays as a Mirror of Their Time

Key dates

  • 1572 — Vagabonds Act applied to actors — needed noble patronage to avoid arrest
  • 1583 — Queen's Men formed — Elizabeth's personal company of players
  • 1590s — Height of Elizabethan theatre — Shakespeare, Marlowe, Jonson

Key people

  • William Shakespeare — Playwright — plays reflected Elizabethan concerns: power, loyalty, usurpation
  • Christopher Marlowe — Playwright — Doctor Faustus, Tamburlaine; controversial; died young 1593
  • Edmund Tilney — Master of the Revels 1579–1610 — censored all plays; a form of government control

Key terms

  • Master of the Revels — Government censor of plays — approved scripts; removed political references
  • Puritan opposition — Puritans opposed theatre — sinful, waste of time, brought crowds together
  • Plague closures — Theatres closed during plague outbreaks — frequent in Elizabethan London
  • Lord Chamberlain's Men — Shakespeare's company — patronised by Lord Chamberlain

Key events

  • Queen's Men formed (1583) — At the time: Elizabeth directly patronised theatre — Long term: gave theatre royal respectability
  • Puritan opposition — At the time: theatres banned within City of London — Long term: theatres moved to Southwark (outside City authority)

Key figures

  • ~15 — Playing companies in London in the 1590s
  • ~20,000 — Londoners attending theatre each week in the 1590s

Why Did Poverty Increase in the 16th Century

Key dates

  • 1530s–1590s — Enclosures accelerated — common land converted to sheep pasture
  • 1558–1603 — Population growth — more mouths, same food supply
  • 1594–97 — Four consecutive harvest failures — famine conditions in parts of England

Key people

  • Elizabethan government — Recognised the problem but saw poverty through a moral lens — deserving vs undeserving poor

Key terms

  • Enclosures — Converting common land to private sheep pasture — displaced peasant farmers
  • Vagabond — Unemployed wanderer — feared by authorities as a source of disorder
  • Sturdy beggars — Those seen as able-bodied but choosing not to work — treated harshly
  • Impotent poor — Those genuinely unable to work (sick, elderly) — deserving of charity
  • Harvest failure — Crop failure causing food shortages — a key driver of 1590s poverty crisis

Key events

  • Enclosures (16th C) — At the time: profitable for landowners — Long term: created a landless, mobile poor population
  • Harvest failures (1594–97) — At the time: famine conditions — Long term: forced government to finally codify the Poor Laws (1601)

Key figures

  • ~2.8 million — Population of England in 1558 — rose to ~4 million by 1603
  • ~10% — Rise in food prices per decade — outpacing wages
  • 4 — Successive harvest failures 1594–97 — catastrophic for the poor

How Did Elizabeth’s Government Deal with the Poor

Key dates

  • 1563 — Statute of Artificers — regulated wages and apprenticeships
  • 1572 — Vagabonds Act — harsh punishment for sturdy beggars; JPs to levy a poor rate
  • 1576 — Provision for poor relief — houses of correction for able-bodied poor
  • 1601 — Elizabethan Poor Law — codified all previous measures; fundamental until 1834

Key people

  • Elizabethan government — Pragmatic approach — recognised poverty was structural, not just moral failure

Key terms

  • Poor Law — 1601 — codified the Elizabethan approach; local parishes responsible for their own poor
  • Impotent poor — Genuinely unable to work (old, sick) — given relief (money, food)
  • Able-bodied poor — Could work but unemployed — given work in houses of correction
  • Sturdy beggars — Chose not to work — punished, whipped, returned to their parish
  • Overseers of the Poor — Parish officials responsible for implementing the Poor Law

Key events

  • Poor Law 1601 — At the time: codified Elizabethan poor relief — Long term: remained the basis of English poor relief until the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834

Key figures

  • 1601 — Poor Law divided the poor into three categories — impotent poor, able-bodied poor, idle poor

The Globe (historic environment)

Site Study - The Globe (1)

Key dates

  • 1576 — The Theatre — first purpose-built playhouse; James Burbage
  • 1599 — Globe Theatre built — using timber from The Theatre
  • 1613 — Globe destroyed by fire
  • 1614 — Globe rebuilt
  • 1642 — Puritans close all theatres
  • 1997 — Shakespeare's Globe opened — modern reconstruction near original site

Key people

  • James Burbage — Built The Theatre 1576 — his sons built the Globe
  • William Shakespeare — The Globe's resident playwright — his plays were written for this space

Key terms

  • Site study — AQA requires analysis of a historic site — the Globe is the Elizabethan site study
  • Globe Theatre — 1599 — Bankside, Southwark; open-air; thrust stage
  • Groundlings — Standing audience in the yard
  • Historic site — A place that can be analysed for evidence of the past

Key events

  • Globe Theatre built (1599) — At the time: new home for the Lord Chamberlain's Men — Long term: became the defining symbol of Elizabethan theatre
  • Modern Globe (1997) — At the time: opened for audiences — Long term: allows analysis of what the original experience was like

Key figures

  • ~3,000 — Capacity of the Globe — standing and seated
  • 1 penny — Entry for groundlings — accessible to all
  • ~200 — Metres from the original site to the current Shakespeare's Globe

Site Study - The Globe (2)

Key dates

  • 1599 — Original Globe built — evidence of Elizabethan theatre and society
  • 1989 — Archaeological excavation of original Globe site — physical evidence
  • 1997 — Modern Globe opened — used to understand performance and space

Key people

  • Sam Wanamaker — American actor who campaigned for the Globe's reconstruction from the 1970s
  • William Shakespeare — The plays written for this space reveal what Elizabethan audiences valued

Key terms

  • Change — What the Globe shows about Elizabethan society changing — the key site study concept
  • Continuity — What stayed the same — cross-class audiences; outdoor entertainment
  • Feature analysis — Looking at physical features of the Globe and what they tell us about Elizabethan society

Key events

  • Archaeological excavation (1989) — At the time: found remains of original Globe — Long term: provided physical evidence to inform the reconstruction
  • Shakespeare's Globe opened (1997) — At the time: new research tool — Long term: allows historians to test ideas about performance

Key figures

  • 20 sides — Polygon shape of the Globe — architectural evidence
  • ~30 metres — Diameter of the Globe

Site Study - The Globe (3)

Key dates

  • 1599 — Globe built — the physical space tells us about Elizabethan culture
  • 1613 — Globe burns — a cannon during Henry VIII; even the fire tells us something

Troubles abroad: Drake & the Armada

Why Did Drake Circumnavigate the Globe

Key dates

  • 1568 — Hawkins and Drake humiliated at San Juan de Ulúa by Spain — personal motivation for Drake
  • 1577 — Drake sets off on circumnavigation — secretly authorised by Elizabeth
  • 1580 — Drake returns to Plymouth — knighted by Elizabeth on the Golden Hind

Key people

  • Francis Drake — 1540–96 — privateer and explorer; second person to circumnavigate the globe
  • Elizabeth I — Secretly authorised the voyage — then publicly honoured Drake; a calculated act of provocation towards Spain

Key terms

  • Circumnavigation — Sailing all the way around the world — only the second time it had been done
  • Privateering — Officially unofficial piracy — Drake raided Spanish ships with Elizabeth's unofficial blessing
  • Golden Hind — Drake's ship — renamed mid-voyage to honour his patron Sir Christopher Hatton
  • New Albion — Drake's name for the California coast — claimed for England

Key events

  • Drake's circumnavigation (1577–80) — At the time: massively profitable raid on Spanish shipping — Long term: humiliated Spain; strained Anglo-Spanish relations; contributed to the Armada
  • Drake knighted (1580) — At the time: Elizabeth rewarded Drake openly — Long term: Spain demanded his punishment; Elizabeth's knighting was a deliberate provocation

Key figures

  • 3 years — Duration of the voyage — 1577 to 1580
  • ~£160,000 — Value of loot Drake brought back — roughly 2 years of Crown revenue
  • 59 — Crew who survived out of ~164 who set out

Why Was Drake’s Circumnavigation an Achievement

Key dates

  • 1577–80 — Drake's circumnavigation — navigational, military and political achievement
  • 1580 — Drake returns — knighted; his navigational charts used by later sailors

  • Ferdinand Magellan — Portuguese explorer — first circumnavigation 1519–22; died in the Philippines
  • Elizabeth I — Knighted Drake personally on the Golden Hind at Deptford — a deliberate diplomatic statement

  • Navigation — Drake used his own skill — stars, currents, charts
  • Privateering — The commercial purpose — raiding Spanish ships in the Pacific
  • Golden Hind — The surviving ship — preserved at Deptford as a tourist attraction until the 1660s
